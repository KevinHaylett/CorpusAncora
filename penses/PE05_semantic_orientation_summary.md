## Core Claim

Some nouns are not neutral symbolic objects. They are oriented trajectories temporarily compressed into object-form. In the closing formulation: **"Some words are not scalar meanings. They are vector meanings."** The paper introduces the concept of the *direction-bearing noun* — a noun whose semantic function cannot be fully recovered unless the directional relation, complement, inverse, or counter-geometry that stabilises it is also retained. PE05 extends the observation from PE04 (where B was shown to compress handed rotational charge-mass dynamics) into language as a whole, proposing a formal notation, a philosophical grounding via Wittgenstein, and a practical research programme using LLMs as semantic interferometers.

## §1 — Purpose

PE05 is a direct adjunct to PE04. The earlier charge-mass and B discussion treated the right-hand rule not as a mnemonic but as part of the externalised representation required to recover the handed geometry compressed into electromagnetic equations. This note extends that observation into language more generally: it asks whether some words — especially nouns — are not semantically complete unless their directional, oppositional, or geometric orientation is retained.

The central claim is stated in two forms:

> Some nouns are not neutral symbolic objects. They are oriented trajectories temporarily compressed into object-form.

> A noun may look static in grammar while remaining directional in semantic phase space.

---

## §2 — Direction as Inherently Relational

Direction differs from ordinary noun-like compression. A direction is always a direction *with respect to something*: a frame, a body, a coordinate system, a prior motion, a convention, or a boundary. It is not easily reduced to a neutral object.

This suggests that some meanings carry orientation as an inherent part of their semantic function: not merely located in semantic space but having a handedness, polarity, or directional asymmetry *within* that space.

Examples of oriented pairs treated as evidence:
left/right; clockwise/anticlockwise; source/sink; positive/negative; inside/outside; certainty/doubt; matter/antimatter; charge/sign; B and the right-hand rule; measurement/uncertainty.

These are not merely pairs of words. They are paired or oriented semantic structures whose meanings are stabilised through a relation that cannot be removed without damaging the word.

---

## §3 — Direction-Bearing Nouns

Working definition:

> A direction-bearing noun is a noun whose semantic function cannot be fully recovered unless the directional relation or counter-geometry that stabilises it is also retained.

Such nouns are not false or to be abandoned. Their object-form is a compression. When the compressed orientation is forgotten, the noun may be mistaken for a *thing* rather than a *trajectory-bearing relation*. This is especially important in scientific language, where nouns that began as convenient compressions of measurement can harden into primitives — after which the directional structure that originally stabilised them is hidden inside equations, conventions, signs, diagrams, gestures, or boundary conditions.

---

## §4 — The Grammatical Gender Analogy

Grammatical gender provides a structural analogy: noun-like forms can carry features that are not optional decorations. The grammar requires that the noun be handled according to its assigned orientation.

The taxonomy of orientation types:
- **Gender in grammar**: linguistic orientation-marker
- **Handedness in electromagnetism**: geometric orientation-marker
- **Polarity in logic**: conceptual orientation-marker
- **Matter/antimatter**: physical-symbolic orientation-marker
- **Certainty/doubt**: epistemic or Wittgensteinian orientation-marker

The point is not that these structures are the same. The point is that noun-like symbols can carry structural orientation, and that such orientation may be necessary for the noun to function correctly within a symbolic trajectory.

---

## §5 — Matter, Antimatter, and Counter-Geometry

The word *antimatter* is already directional at the lexical level: the prefix *anti-* implies against, opposite, counter, reversed, reflected, or facing. A decompressed reading avoids treating antimatter as a hostile or negative substance. Instead it reads antimatter as *matter under a different admissible geometry* — a trajectory in which some sign, charge relation, measurement convention, or orientation has been reversed.

The pattern generalises: a concept is stabilised, then a counter-concept is introduced. The second term is not merely another object; it is generated by an *operation* on the first. The semantic structure is therefore not scalar but relational.

---

## §6 — Wittgenstein: Certainty Requires Doubt

Wittgenstein's reflections on certainty (*On Certainty*) provide the strongest philosophical example. Certainty is meaningful only within a language-game in which doubt is possible. If doubt has no foothold, certainty itself loses its ordinary function.

Certainty is thus a direction-bearing noun. It carries doubt as its counter-geometry. It points away from doubt, but it also depends on doubt for its boundary:

> Certainty is not merely the absence of doubt. It is a stabilised symbolic condition whose meaning is partly generated by the possible presence of doubt.

Broader principle:

> Some symbolic objects are not semantically complete unless their opposite, inverse, complement, or orientation-rule is retained.

For Functional Symbolic Trajectories, this prevents premature noun-stabilisation: the word that appears as a single object may in fact be a compressed pair, a polarity, or an oriented substructure.

## §8 — Semantic Dipoles and Oriented Substructures: Formal Notation

An ordinary compressed noun: **w ∈ S** (word w in semantic space S).

A direction-bearing noun requires additional structure:

$$w_d = (w,\, \vec{o},\, \bar{w},\, C,\, H,\, \delta)$$

where:
- **w** = the visible word or symbol
- **→o** = its orientation or directionality
- **w̄** = a counterword, complement, inverse, or shadow term
- **C** = the consensus or admissibility condition under which the word is used
- **H** = the historical or provenance layer of usage
- **δ** = the uncertainty or unresolved semantic spread

The visible noun may not be semantically complete on its own. The missing orientation may be carried elsewhere in the language, the grammar, the equation, the diagram, the apparatus, or the reader's embodied interpretive convention.

**Semantic dipole notation:**

$$D(w) = (w^+,\, w^-,\, \vec{o}_{+-})$$

where w⁺ and w⁻ indicate paired symbolic poles whose meaning arises through the oriented relation between them (not positive/negative in a numerical sense).

Worked examples:

$$D(\text{certainty}) = (\text{certainty},\, \text{doubt},\, \vec{o})$$

$$D(\text{inside}) = (\text{inside},\, \text{outside},\, \vec{o})$$

$$D(B) = (B,\, RH \circ R[\mathcal{C}_{cm}],\, \vec{o}_{RH})$$

The important point is not the notation but the claim: **semantic stability may require hidden orientation**.

## §9 — Transformers and Hidden Handedness

A large language model does not store words as isolated dictionary entries. Its trained representations encode relational structure across vast numbers of contexts, learning transformations, oppositions, analogies, conventions, and direction-like movements through representation space. The model may therefore be said to carry handedness in its weights — meaning not a single readable "handedness neuron" but that the trained representational geometry may contain local asymmetries corresponding to handed, directional, polarised, or oppositional semantic structures.

When the model encounters a symbol such as B, it may activate not only "magnetic field" but also: curl, right-hand rule, compass deflection, axial vector, pseudovector, circulation, current, torque, charge motion, cross product, and directional force — a larger semantic cloud than the formal noun alone presents.

**Key methodological proposal:**

> The language model may be treated as a *semantic interferometer*. A word, symbol, or equation is used as a perturbation. The response reveals nearby attractor structures in semantic space. If the same hidden orientation repeatedly appears across different prompts, framings, and models, then the word may carry an irreducible directional component.

This does not make the model an authority. It makes it an instrument for probing the geometry of stabilised language. The danger of over-reading is noted: some responses reflect frequency, style, or training distribution rather than irreducible semantic geometry. Any use of a model as semantic instrument should include repeated trials, prompt variation, cross-model comparison, and where possible, embedding-level analysis.

## §10 — Possible Experimental Programme

Six probe experiments are proposed:
1. Ask about B without mentioning handedness → observe whether curl, rotation, or right-hand rule reappears
2. Ask about curl without mentioning the right-hand rule → observe whether handed coordinate conventions reappear
3. Ask about certainty without mentioning doubt → observe whether doubt, hinge, ground, or boundary terms reappear
4. Ask about antimatter without mentioning reversal → observe whether charge conjugation, sign, opposite, or temporal reversal language reappears
5. Ask about measurement without mentioning uncertainty → observe whether error, calibration, instrument, or boundary terms reappear
6. Ask about grammatical gender without mentioning orientation → observe whether agreement, classification, or structural marking reappears

Potential measurements: repeated emergence of counterwords; repeated emergence of directional verbs; repeated emergence of handedness or polarity terms; sensitivity to prompt reversal; differences across languages with and without grammatical gender; differences across symbolic domains (physics, logic, law, theology, ordinary speech); embedding-space displacement between a noun and its counter-noun; local clustering of diagrams, gestures, equations, and explanatory conventions around a word.

## §12 — Future Directions

Five directions:
1. **Catalogue direction-bearing nouns** — candidates include certainty, doubt, proof, truth, charge, field, force, matter, antimatter, left, right, source, sink, inside, outside, measurement, uncertainty, observer, boundary
2. **Distinguish neutral from oriented object-compressions** — the former may function as temporary nouns; the latter require their orientation-rule to remain recoverable
3. **Extend the charge-mass/B analysis** into a systematic study of hidden handedness in electromagnetic language; first targets remain v×B (Lorentz force) and ∇×B (Ampere-Maxwell)
4. **Test LLMs as semantic interferometers** — same terms probed across different prompts, models, and languages; goal is identifying persistent counter-structures and directional attractors
5. **Connect to the FST framework** — direction-bearing nouns as cases where the visible symbol carries an unreduced trajectory, and where meaning is lost if the trajectory is flattened into a static noun

## §13 — Closing Formulation

> Some nouns are not semantic points but oriented substructures in language-space. Their apparent object-form hides directional geometry, polarity, handedness, or counter-relation. When such nouns are used in science, philosophy, or mathematics, the missing orientation may reappear as a rule, sign, convention, diagram, gesture, boundary condition, or interpretive procedure. A language model may preserve traces of these hidden orientations in its learned representation, making it useful as an instrument for detecting where language has compressed trajectory into noun-form.

Or more simply:

> **Some words are not scalar meanings. They are vector meanings.**
