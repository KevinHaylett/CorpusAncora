Part I — The Word and Its Authority
The Storyteller
It seems to me that if you’re here, you value words. In which case, I’m here to tell a story. But before starting, perhaps, by knowing a little about me, it may help you decide if the story is worth reading. And that begs a question: how can you do that?

To start with, you may need some provenance. By provenance, I simply mean the history of these words: where they came from, and the pathway by which they came. Perhaps, then, by seeing the path of the person placing them before you, you may be better placed to decide to follow it—or not.

At the time of writing, I am 63. My first degree was in biomedical electronics. I followed a career of science in medicine and completed my PhD in 2000, over twenty-five years ago. Working as a clinical scientist in the UK’s NHS, I specialised in the measurement of complex information. I read, built things, repaired things, measured things, experimented, and listened to the world.

On Time Travelling
This story involves time travelling and perhaps is not so easy to tell, but I’ll try and do my best. It’s not a quick bish-bash-boof, here it is—next, now move on. No, it’s a tale across time that we will travel together.

We’ll first travel backwards into what may now appear as rather arcane times. However, the people who lived then, like us, wouldn’t have thought of their lives in that way. We will begin within the sixteenth century and follow a path towards the present.

This is a drama. A drama about people. And it is also about words, authority, politics and, at times, life and death. It’s about who was permitted to tell a story, who could pass it on, and what happens when different arrangements of words offer different ways of seeing the world.

A Story Is Carried by the Words We Use
Importantly, the words we use today did not simply arrive in 2026. They carry a history, and their own provenance. They have moved through books, arguments, translations, institutions and generations of readers. Their meanings have shifted as the paths around them have changed. In that sense, the language we now inhabit is a path, a trajectory, and we’re already living inside the story we are about to examine.

By now, perhaps, you’ve assessed the storyteller. Importantly, I claim no authority. I am not the authority. I’m just a storyteller, and the story must be found in the words placed before you and the pathways the words enable you to follow.

If you want authority, it is yours to give, not mine. You may place it in an organisation, an institution, the Crown, God, the Tao, a scientific body, a university, or perhaps nowhere in particular. The Book by My Side
So we shall begin with a man called Ramus and a very small book.

The book beside me as I write is an example of the many books that began to move across Europe after the arrival of printing. That movement of words and books is a large part of this story. By the sixteenth century, the printing press was no longer a curiosity, it was busy rattling away across Europe’s cities and becoming a workhorse of change. Printers, translators, booksellers and readers were becoming a part everyday life that was considered modern.

Like now, they were exciting times. Previously unimaginable and growing pathways of new words were changing how people could think. Sequences of words could be shared not simply by word of mouth, but by books that people could own, hold and refer to with precision.

Where Are ‘We’?
It seems to me that we must try to place ourselves within those times. Europe was moving through what we now call the Renaissance and the Reformation, and towards what we later called the Enlightenment. These names are perhaps convenient labels applied afterwards. Yet the people living through those years did not wake in the morning and announce that they were participating in a period drama. Just as we encounter AI, they encountered changes in their own lives: a sermon spoken differently, a book appearing in another language, an old text returning in a new edition, or a never-heard-before argument travelling from one city to another.

The Authority of the Written Word
For many people, the most familiar encounter with an authoritative written word came through religion. The words were often heard rather than privately read: scripture, liturgy, preaching and repeated phrases.

The words and language were held by the institution that preserved and interpreted them. When a book spoke of God and the heavens, the heavens were not merely a distant metaphor. They were the sky, the stars, the ordered creation around the Earth, and part of the world in which people lived. And ‘God’, the word, made that world intelligible.

The meaning we now give to such words has travelled a long way. We inhabit them in a different way. Our sense of the reality of ‘God’ and the heavens has changed. Much of the language of the past is invisible to us: we have moved on, and have more words and more ideas. Heaven is now a more remote and distant place, somewhere angels may live, not as people but as ‘spirits’ in some untouchable ethereal world that we now conjure up in our imagination.

The Authority of Language
Part of our story will concern how authority moved through that changing language: how words once held within a religious understanding began to sit beside words drawn from other texts, other times and other sources of authority. This change was not sudden, religious words and authority did not simply disappear. The same printing presses carrying new arguments also carried Bibles, devotional works and fierce religious disputes. However, what changed was that new, never-before-seen pathways became more visible.

Ancient Authority
The printers worked hard; earlier Greek and Roman texts began to circulate in newly edited and translated forms. The ancient texts were now new again, printed with new ink on fresh cotton-rag paper. The older words brought forth new discussions and new arguments.

In monasteries, these discussions were not new. Christian scholars had preserved, interpreted and disputed the earlier texts, however, they had also been their gatekeepers for centuries. In the 16th and 17the century the ancient texts were increasingly available beyond the monastery to those wealthy enough to afford a book. The ancient texts could then be disputed beyond the monastery doors. Texts from before the birth of Christianity were once again alive and being compared and measured. Both books and arguments could be carried between universities and cities. The words could be read and argued about in ways that had not previously been possible.

A Time of Fracture
The deep fracture occurring during the Reformation cannot be placed upon printing alone. As in any age, change comes in many forms. People do not go to war merely because a machine produces pages. Yet once different arrangements of words could be reproduced and transported on pages, ideas and disagreements could travel with them.

To those outside the monastery, a book had often only meant “the words of God”. Now a book could hold words that came before their Christian God. ,The words, with written authority, could be arranged along pathways that no longer had to begin and end with God. People could begin to think with different words following different patterns of thought.

We can begin to see that disputes over language were no constrained by the monastery or university walls. They words within the older philosophies and stories could be translated, purchased, carried home and read again, in your own language.

The new books gathered more readers as reading spread. Readers become communities. The authority of the written word was no longer the sole domain of religion and the monastery. Communities become institutions, churches, and political movements. And sometimes, as a result, people grasp and find different meaning, and ultimately opposite sides engage in war as they fight for the emaning held in the words.

The Economy of Knowledge
However it must be remembered, just as it is now, printing was a business. Books cost money to produce. Paper and ink have to be made, the press had to be run, and the type had to be made and set. Printers needed people to buy their books. And then, just as it is now, writers, translators and publishers tried to make books that would attract attention and find readers.

A book had to present itself as valuable. It needed a title, a name and a reason why a prospective reader should pay for the words inside it. The market did not sit outside the history of ideas; it became the means by which some words travelled and others did not.

Consider that for a moment, and how the authority of the written word is being given. A latin name bestows the authority of education, of seriousness, even though the translation is in English.

The printing itself holds book’s provenance. The object in my hand was manufactured in 1966 and yet the printed text travelled from 1574. The words are a translation of those of a French scholar, Peter Ramee who wrote, taught, argued, revised his books and died before this English version appeared.

Between Peter and myself stands a translator, printers, booksellers, libraries, collectors, a facsimile publisher and nearly five centuries of readers. This book cannot give me unmediated access to the mind of the man we now call Ramus. Yet it gives me a surviving symbolic pathway and continue our story.

Where We Have Arrived
We have not yet reached modern science. However,r we have reached something upon which modern science would depend; the reproducible movement of words beyond the person who first spoke or arranged them. Books such as those of Ramus gave us a method where we no longer have to be in the same room as the teacher. The exposition can be compressed into a small portable object that cane be purchased, carried away, and activated again in the life of another reader.

A printed book does not merely preserve thought. It selects, arranges and reproduces a pathway through thought. A reader can follow the pathway without meeting its originator. And at the same time the neighbouring disputes, conversations and rejected alternatives became less visible. A book shifts the authority of a speaker towards the authority of the surviving words themselves.

Part II — The Division of the Word
Petrus Ramus, Martyr
The book’s title tells us something before we’ve read a single argument:

The Moste Excellent Philosopher P. Ramus Martyr.

These words prepare us to meet the writer. The author is introduced with considerable purpose and intent. Ramus is excellent, a philosopher and also a martyr. There is perhaps an implication that he died for these words. The book’s title begins by telling us how its author should be received — with authority.

No longer just Pierre de la Ramée in the scholarly Latin of the time, he became Petrus Ramus. This latinisation of his name was common, it claimed authority, placing Ramus on a pedestal, a learned pathway high above vernacular speech. Pierre’s identity was established from the first words. Every word matters when claiming authority.

Ramus The Teacher and Philosopher
In his daily life Ramus was a philosopher, a teacher and a reformer. He wrote many books rather than a single final statement and his works carried his ideas widely across Europe. His ideas spread, as written words were revised, republished, condensed and carried forward. The printing presses carried the beat and rhythm of his sentences.

Teaching People How to Think
One question concerned Ramus deeply, it was extraordinary and practical:

how might people be taught to think?

Consider what Ramus was saying: people could not think - and they needed teaching to be able to think at all. Ramus is also telling us more, he’s judging the people and suggests that not only does he have a method, but is better than how people usually think.

Importantly, having a method directs what is possible. It tells a student where to begin, what to separate, what belongs inside a subject and what may be left outside. Ramus’s question suggests there is a best way of thinking:

Tis is how to think—this is how you should think.

The Power of a Method
Once we have a written method it can enters a university or school, it can be passed from teacher to pupil. And upon teaching a previously unfamiliar arrangement of words begins to feel natural in the mind of the student. And, if no other method is available, it may become the only way to think. From there we can see it becoming the ‘natural’ way of thinking and perhaps, it is still being followed in these words.

Ramus the Martyr
Ramus lived during violent religious and political conflict. He became a Protestant and was killed in Paris in 1572 during the St Bartholomew’s Day massacre. We don’t know the details of his death, yet the word martyr is being used to add value to the authority of the book. His life, the killing and the logic now travel together. From a few words on a title page we find the creation of a mythology.

Roland’s title title tells us the work is newly translated and, in several places, corrected “after the mynde of the Author”. Roland, as translator, is not only offering translation; he is also claiming to carry Ramus’s intended meaning as if he could read the mind of a martyr who could no longer speak for himself.

Touching the Past
When holding the book, what strikes me is that it feels, and is, small—a little over a hundred pages. That smallness matters. Roland compressed a much larger body of work into a book to be held, carried and read.

Compression is a modern word. In the sixteenth century there were no compressed digital files, no compressed video and no compressed photographs. Mathematical theories and symbols describing compression were hundreds of years away. Nevertheless, details were selected, arranged and reduced as Roland judged what would make his book useful, saleable and valued. We can see that this made Roland’s translation more accessible. Yet, it had a cost as perhaps some of the finer details were laid aside for brevity.

The Value of a Method of Thinking
However, imagine what such an object might have meant to its owner in 1574. A person could acquire a small book which offered, in English, a way of arranging thought: a guide to thinking clearly. Unlike the thousands of self-help books we now have, this was one of the early printed guides of its kind available to those who could afford a copy. Roland’s little book was, in a limited but important sense, the AI of the sixteenth century.

A holder of this book did not need to meet Ramus, travel across Europe or attend a university to hear a lecture. They did not even need him to be alive. The printed words on the page could activate the method again and again, in another place and in another person. This was a miracle of the then modern technology and perhaps seen as a revelation. However, for those in the sixteenth century it was perhaps more consequential: a reproducible intellectual method had become available to any who could read and afford a book. Education had become and a valuable commodity one could buy.

A Method of Division
So what did the readers buy? How did Ramus tell us to think ‘logically’?

When we read The Logike, we find that Ramist logic starts by dividing dialectic. Dialectic was known a method of reasoning that explores truth through the interaction of opposing or contradictory ideas.

Ramus tells us to organise arguments into principal parts: invention and judgement.

On reading, we follow the method as Ramus explains that invention concerns the finding of arguments, while judgement concerns their arrangement and disposition.

Ramus shows, by worked examples, that from these divisions further divisions can be made, and a subject can be worked down through branching distinctions into an ordered form. That is what we may now think of as a branching tree. Of course, that is itself a substantive simplification.

However, the key to the method is order and layered division. We can perhaps see this as a measured, directed flow. Logic in the hands of Ramus becomes the flow from larger parts to smaller parts in a direction, for example from top to bottom: from the king to the serf, from a brook to the sea. He shows how we can apply this method across domains, division, layering, and ordering.

Following his method gives us language that we readily see as everyday thought. We may not even consider it a method. However, it was not simply everyday language. And this method, this use of language is Ramist logic. I suspect that it feels familiar, every day, it is ‘logical’. However, it may be a mistake to think that language was always structured, taught or authorised in precisely this way.

Before Ramus, God was a principal source of meaning across the observed world. However, we can see that Ramist logic permits God to be placed in a different position and no longer requires God to be present at every level of an explanation. This enables us to see ‘God’ in a new light. In addition, ‘God’ begins slowly to move outside the flow of ‘cause and effect’ that travels with the branching tree—another of Ramus’s structures of logic.

Of course, Ramus did not invent division itself. People had classified and distinguished long before him, and the classical traditions upon which he worked already contained elaborate systems of logic and rhetoric. What Ramus offered was a particularly teachable arrangement that could be placed in a small book and repeated in a lesson. And then the method could be applied to new fields.

For me, this is where the book beside me begins to open into the larger story. This is because by naming divisions we can communicate with finer resolution. Only by separate one thing from another, can we examine a relationships between the parts. In addition we can see the connections that may be made made between the parts.

This is the beginning of what I’ve come to think of as the Great Parting: not a single event on a particular day, and not something caused by Ramus alone, but a growing confidence that the world could be made intelligible by partitioning it into ordered symbolic parts. The success of the method gave it momentum. What could be divided could be placed in a book; what could be placed in a book could be taught; and what could be taught could become an institution and the story of the world.

The Consequences of Division
Ramus’s goal was to educate and he believed in his philosophy. For me, it tells me that he’d been using his own methodology and considered the results useful. And having found it useful he wanted to share that success and make it available for others. This was a process of re-forming how we thought. To me this his is important as Ramus was following the questions from his time using the best words and instruments he had available, in a method he had developed by a process of application. That of division, hierarchy, and ordering via a process


The path of his ideas and those of other thinkers into our current language was made through accumulation of successes. The iterative success of the methods and language when was measured against the world to ‘make sense’.

It seems that once a partition becomes established and found to be useful, later writers can begin from that starting point without repeating the argument that created it. The partition becomes part of the supporting language. It disappears into the apparently ordinary structure of a textbook, a curriculum or a sentence. It becomes a hidden ‘reality’ assumed within our language.

From this perspective, the book of logic then begins to resemble the earlier religious books in one important respect: it arrives bearing authority. The authority is no longer necessarily announced as the “Word of God”; it may now be the authority of logic, method, reason, experiment or mathematics. However, as readers, we are still being offered an arrangement of symbols and told, sometimes openly and sometimes quietly, that this is the proper and ‘right’ pathway through which understanding can proceed.

The Naming of Things
Importantly a process of dividing creates a boundary in language. On one side of the boundary is this; on the other is that. Then once the distinction is repeated and shared, each side can be given a name. This is the transition from a process that creates new nouns. That is to say compact symbolics object which can be placed into a sentence without reconstructing the full path by which the division was first made.

The Power of Nouns
Nouns are extraordinarily useful. Consider how difficult it would be if every time we spoke every word required us to repeat its entire history, of the process, that enables the word to be created. However, the usefulness of the name appears to hold a tension within new sequence of words. For example, we start using a noun to hold together a selected set of observations, and start imagining the noun to hold a ‘thing’ that has been usefully divided and separated from everything around it.

The world beyond the symbol did not physically separate. Rather the symbol was created to enable the sentence to become possible. The ‘branch’, and the ‘trunk’, once names are still a part of the ‘tree’. And when we seperate them, in language, and in the measurable world, we need new words to gain the story. We cut, the branch, and then the tree. Here we can see the noun as a symbolic holding place, supported by the surrounding pathways through which it acquires meaning. When the pathways are forgotten, the name appears as the thing itself, an symbols that has lost it’s provenance.

No Person Stands Alone
Ramian Logic was just one of many growing pathways of thought and was not a movement in one direction, followed and taught by only one person. Our wider language grew the desire for clarity. And that clarity to describe the world grew through dictionaries, catalogues, natural histories, collections, schools, universities and the practical success of recognising and distinguishing repeatable patterns.

Plants, animals, minerals, organs, diseases, instruments and motions could all be given names. Each new name enabled finer comparison. Each finer comparison enabled new practices, and those practices supported still finer distinctions. Ramus was just one of many pointing the way to finer and more ordered language.

And looking back we may see that the difficulty was not that the distinctions were necessarily wrong. It seems to me was that the continuing world from which they had been made could disappear behind them. We have come to inhabit an increasingly fine landscape of nouns and treat the divisions between the nouns as though they are the original boundaries of the world before the measurements are made and symbols created.

Where We Have Arrived
We can now see that Ramus showed us how a field may be divided into ordered and named parts and printing has enabled the method to feed forward. And maybe now we can get a sense how after repetition a constructed partition can disappear into apparently ordinary thought.

In this story, the world has yet to become mathematical. We can see that first it has become increasingly nameable. The next movement in our story is different as it does not principally divide the world into nouns. Rather it creates a series of relationships by teaching the reader to do something quite extra ordinary as we will see how we’ve been taught to remove the material extent of the marks from which relations are constructed. To follow that second pathway, we must travel backwards in time before travelling forwards yet again.

Part III — The Point Without Extent
An Older Book Returns
History lets us measure that Euclid lived many centuries before Ramus. So the provenance of hour book tell this is not a simple story of A leading to Bin. Rather than Ramus simply following Euclid, Euclid is returned to after Ramus. Perhaps, what matters here is that old texts can return within new conditions and provide a new pathway of thought when placed beside other methods, other books and other institutions.

The Elements possibly one of the most influential mathematics books of all time, had travelled through manuscripts, commentaries, translations and different cultures long before the European printing press.

However, with printing, editions of Euclid could circulate more widely and with a newly repeatable arrangement of words, diagrams and propositions. Asa result of these printed books, An ancient geometrical pathway of thought became part of the working symbolic equipment of early-modern Europe. And as we have learned books have been placed in a position where they are given great authority.

This is one of the peculiarities of our time travelling. An older text can arrive in a later world and participate in making something new. The age of the sentences does not determine the age in which the sentences can be re-constructed.

Euclid’s Opening Instructions
Going all the way back to ancient Greek texts may seem a dry academic journey into forgotten history with little bearing on the world of words we now inhabit. How many people are taught Euclid directly. And here’s a clue, the indirect teaching of Euclid is ubiquitous. Tis in every corner of modern science and mathematical thinking. Every trajectory of these basin of thought, carry the provenance of Euclid. Without Euclid’s methods, mathematics and science as we know it would be unavailable for us to think or follow. And Euclid offered a very special type of word-magic which we now call imagination.

Euclid Creates the Geometers World of Imagination
An early 17th Century book of Euclid sits on my shelf. This is a book that formed the basis of much of modern day mathematics. So maybe its importance cannot be overstated. It has set the pathway from which the language we use, now, here, in this text has travelled

Euclid’s Elements begins with definitions. In a familiar English translation, and states:

a Geometer’s point has no part

a Geometer’s line is breadthless length

a Geometer’s surface has length and breadth only, and the boundaries of a surface are lines

These statements are now so deeply embedded within mathematics they pass almost unnoticed. Yet perhaps we should pause, what exactly have we been asked to accept?

Where the Measured World Becomes Imagination
If we place a point upon a page, no matter how fine the pencil, or sharp the instrument, the point has extent. Under magnification it will have a boundary, irregularities, a width and an area. If we press more heavily it may becomes larger, deform the paper and its edge may alter. A mark may be extremely small in relation to the diagram, but it is never that which has no part.

If we continue and draw a line; the line also has measurable extent. It has breadth, however narrow. Whether graphite, ink, a scored groove, illuminated pixels or some other material registration, the line can be measured. It has a beginning and an end made by an interaction between an instrument and a surface. It may be straighter or less straight, finer or thicker, darker or lighter, but it is never literally breadthless.

The Great Acceptance
Euclid therefore presents us with a strange and seemingly impossible instruction. We must use the mark, the lines and points on the paper, but we must not treat the mark as the mathematical object. We retain its position and relation while disregarding its physical extent.

Here we can begin to see that the drawn point activates the “idea of a point” without being the point that the definition describes. The drawn line carries the relation while its breadth is removed using Euclid’s method and accepting his aithority.

We can perhaps see this as the beginning of a mass hypnosis, we are being asked and in practice take this step of imagination. For me, these Geometer’s points, lines and surfaces are not merely an approximation or ‘abstraction’, they are absurd. We are being told that the visible dot is a slightly imperfect version of an imagined Geometer’s dot. We are being asked to cross from a mark with extent to an object defined by the absence of that extent. And in this mathematical world of imagination the physical inscription is present, but its material character is excluded from the formal pathway. Our current working mathematical point is still Euclid’s Geometer’s Point. And it is an imagined object with no extent. It now exists as a symbol, declared as that which has been separated from the world of measurement. Yet the Geometer’s point now need a few sentences to explain it sentences that are rarely shown and lead to the symbols loss of provenance.

How Extent Disappears
The disappearance of extent is easy to miss because the diagram remains before us as a visual irony. Our eye sees the triangle, the circle or the intersecting lines. We may have drawn them with a straightedge and compass and the geometrical reasoning follows the visible construction.

Yet every visible element possesses properties that a mathematical proof does not carry. In our measured world within which we interact, the line has a width which the proof treats it as breadthless. The point covers an area which the proof treats it as without parts. And our paper paper bends and expands; the formal plane does not. When we draw our circle with a compass, the compass point makes a small hole in the page; yet the mathematical centre remains dimensionless.

Opening our eyes and measuring the interaction we can see that a diagram therefore performs two tasks at once. It presents a material arrangement to the senses, and it invites the reader to follow a non-material symbolic construction relation that has been constructed by imagination and Euclid’s Geometer’s rules.

We see the extent, but we learn not to include it. Euclid’s rules were passed down from book to book, from teacher to teacher, each claiming the authority of imagination over measured extent. Over hundreds of years this authority has removed the word’s tether to the measured world. We are told that this because mathematics is ‘abstract’.

As we travel further we can see that using Euclid’s Geometer’s points and lines, we can enter a world of godlike exactness. Once breadth and material irregularity have been excluded, two geometrical lines can be described as “exactly equal”. This “exactly equal” is statement of perfection, and can never be confirmed by measured. To make that statement we use Recorde’s equals symbol and in that instance we can forget the journey that gives authority to making wo finite symbols with extent and removes them from the measurable world. And so we come to the statement:

A point can occupy an exact location without itself taking up space. A circle can be described through equal relations from an exact centre. The uncertainty of the hand, ink, compass and page can be ignore in all subsequent proposition.

The Power of Compression and the Removal of Extent
Despite the removal of extent, the mathematics of Euclid and those built upon it have brought all the way to the modern world. Possibly, modern day mathematics is one of the most powerful instruments we have in the world. Bt removing extent, we simplify, we lose complexity, sentences become shorter, words and symbols connect more easily, rules can be made with greater simplicity. New logic can be constructed that no longer depends on measurement and can be bound to symbols that forget the symbols have extent. Relations can be preserved, repeated and examined without reconstructing every physical event through which a diagram is made.

The question is not whether the method works. The question is what has been removed to enable it to work, and what happens when the resulting symbolic object is later returned to the measured world.

Construction and Imagination
The Elements does not consist of definitions alone. It is large tome and Euclid collected many ideas and placed them in 15 books that form a volume. The books includes postulates and constructions including:

how to draw a straight line from any point to any point

how to extend a finite straight line

how to describe a circle with a given centre and distance.

These are actions are all instructions where we are asked to carry out a dynamical operation. Yet every practical construction remains finite. A hand moves; an edge is followed; a compass turns; a mark is registered. We are asked to set aside finite extent of the resulting forms, the errors of construction, the uncertainties of measurement. In this way, Euclid helps us to hold an idea of an object that we can never literally produce.

Perhaps this is part of the illusion. We do not begin with an invisible realm and somehow bring it to the page. We begin with actions that can be performed, marks which can be seen and relations that can be followed. The measured world with extent disappears in the mapping of the marks to new symbols.

We can see in constructing the lines and circles we participate in the construction. And we can see two, pictures in front of us. One is a page of marks with extent an artifact of interactions. The other is of symbols with a Euclidian tether, that claims lets the symbols lose the connection to the measured world. From here we live in language of acceptance of Euclid’s methods.

The First and Second Partings
We can now begin to see two related but distinct movements. Ramus shows how language can divide a field into parts, arrange those parts and direct the path of thought between them. Euclid shows how mathematics can preserve selected relations while removing the extent of the physical symbols through which those relations are encountered.

Ramus divides and names. Euclid abstracts and removes extent. The first gives us a growing landscape of ordered nouns. The second gives us a relational space in which exact operations can proceed without carrying the material uncertainty of every mark.

Neither movement is the work of one person alone. Neither occurred on a single day, and neither was accepted without argument. Ramus drew upon older traditions. Euclid’s definitions had histories, commentators and critics of their own. However, the names provide us with points along the pathway, places where we can pause and see the structure more clearly.

It seems to me that the Great Parting becomes possible when these two methods begin to operate together. A named thing can be assigned a position, a boundary, a quantity or a trajectory. The mathematical relation can then move between the names as though both the named objects and the mathematical pathways were cleanly separated from the continuing field of interaction through which they were first registered.

The success is real. The danger comes later, when the operations disappear from view. The noun begins to look like the thing, and the extentless mathematical relation begins to look like a property already written into the world.

Where We Have Arrived
Ramus taught how a field of study could be divided into named parts. Euclid supplied a mathematical language in which the marks representing relations between those parts could be treated as though they possessed no material uncertainty.

One method organised the names; the other purified the relations. Both depended upon finite marks, human actions and learned readers, but both could eventually conceal those supports. In this way a pathway is now open for the named and measured heavens to replace the heavens as an immediately religious order.

From here we can now travel forwards again, towards the Royal Society, Newton and the compass.

Part IV — The Compass and the World
From the Heavens to Measurement
As travellers in time, we can begin to see the pathway from the sixteenth to the seventeenth century, and how the language of the heavens begins to materially change. The language of ‘God’ does not yet vanish from the story. However, the nature of the ‘heavens’ starts to transform from being dominated by “God” to a language increasingly dominated through partition and measurement.

If we look at the life of Isaac Newton, we find that he didn’t live in today’s secular scientific world. Rather, history shows us that within the 17th and 18th century natural philosophy, theology, mathematics and scriptural interpretation remained deeply intertwined. However, a new kind of authority was gathering strength: the authority of mathematical relations that could be written, calculated and used again.

When Language Shifts
As the language of division arrives, religion itself stars to be examined beyond the monastery walls. As a result new terms such as natural religion begin to appear. Our notions of the words used for religion begin to change course through new divisions via new pathways of thought previously unavailable.

It seems, that new ideas were able to form because ‘God’ no longer occupied the prime position in every proposition. As a result new questions could be asked about how the observed world worked. And with new methods of thinking. new structures of order become visible and even more questions can be asked. The new methods of philosophy were ‘generative’ and highly productive.

In the 17th Century Mathematics Takes Center Stage
Is your story of mathematics a great success, or does it fall away from language?

As we have travelled, you perhaps, are following the words within the frame of your own language and understanding. In which case ‘mathematics’ will be a unique part of your story. For some it’s a rich tale, for others it’s a brief excursion that failed to lead anywhere productive.

We each come to mathematics, not from our mother’s tongue, but from structure and education, culture and society. And that provenance directs our journey. Importantly, when we see the word ‘mathematics’ it has gathered and locked in its own authority. However, examining the nature of the lock is perhaps important; as we need to understand how the lock was built.

In the 16th and 17th century, as books became more popular, not only were there self-help books on methods of thinking, but there new books about how to do mathematics.

Perhaps, the most famous of these are by Robert Recorde. Not only did he publish the 1557 The Whetstone of Witte, where he gave is equals symbol, he also published the Grounde of the Arts. This book, written in English, enabled those beyond the walls of the monasteries and Universities to practice what previously was seen as a dark art. Mathematics was moving from being unseen and mystical into a method, out in the open, that once learned could be now shared and passed onward.

The printed words and mathematical runes could carry the methods beyond the pages of Recorde’s book into the ‘mynde’ of the practitioner. And like the words of ‘God’ written in the bible, another pathway had been compressed, and mathematics acquired the authority of a book.

The Royal Society and the Authority of the King

Now you many or may not know much about the “Royal Society”. It is perhaps, one of the most profound institution that directed the world and language of modern science. It’s full name is: The Royal Society of London for Improving Natural Knowledge.

The first example of claiming of authority is the common shortening where we lose the “London”, and then by implication the authority extends to the wider world.

Reading about the Royal Society is to begin to appreciate how the language of science gained institutional authority. We can then begin to see how profoundly the Society was to influence the language we now use. Here we can see the importance of the new philosophers, not just Ramus, but all the thinkers, makers and writers who were adding their voices.

The Society began in the middle of the 17th century as a small group of people meeting to discuss experiments and matters of natural philosophy. These were dangerous and unsettled times. England had lived through civil war, the execution of Charles I, the Commonwealth and the Protectorate. Following the Restoration of Charles II, the new society sought and received royal authority. The Crown gained a learned institution associated with improvement and knowledge; the Society gained the protection and standing of a royal charter. And the new King had clould claim the support of influential thinkers. Even kings had deep concerns about their authority!

Here we can see how authority shapes the language we are able to use. One important concern of the early Society was the manner in which observations and experiments should be reported. Thomas Sprat’s History of the Royal Society, published in 1667, famously associated the Society with a plainer style, suspicious of rhetorical ornament when describing experimental matters. The declared aim was not to remove language, which would be impossible, but to discipline it so that words appeared to stand more directly beside things and acts.

Another related enterprise appears in the work of John Wilkins, a founding figure of the Royal Society. His Essay towards a Real Character and a Philosophical Language, published in 1668, commissioned by theh Royal Society attempted to arrange knowledge through systematic divisions and to develop characters related to that arrangement. Here again we see the branching of the world into finer and finer symbolic parts.

Of course Sprat, Wilkins and the whole Royal Society were not a a single mind. They often held different opinions. Nevertheless, the institution did hold an agenda and voice. This was of the importance of ‘clear language’, ordered classification, reproducible observation. As a result the naming of things became part of an institutional programme. And that institutional program had the authority of the King.

We perhaps have a sense that this the aspiration was understandable. Because if the names could be stabilised, by consensus different observers might compare their accounts. In speaking we want our words to be understood, and so if the language could be clear and less uncertain, a report might travel more easily. There becomes a path for the natural world to be arranged into increasingly precise classes, knowledge can be begin to accumulate based on a consensus, that is to say a pathway from the past.

Yet the strength of this approach carries a deep concern. To name a living process as a static noun is to create something that can be held as a word but the path and history of the word, its provenance, is left outside the name. The noun allows the observation to travel in a story, in a book, or in article, or research paper. Yet, the word and marking of a distinction and separation, perhaps encourages the reader to forget that the ‘apparent thing’ was produced through an act of distinction, of consensus. And that that occurred via a distance a path that may no longer be visible or even available.

Natural Philosophy Meets Euclid
The path to the 17th century, Locke, Bacon and Newton and the many natural philosophers of that time were texts that were themselves being printed, translated, taught and disputed.

Among the most important was Euclid. Euclid’s geometer’s point without parts and the breadthless line now entered into a world increasingly populated by division as named bodies, motions, substances and forces.

Perhaps you can see the story of our own thought patterns unfolding. Words and language becomes the sequences we consider ‘logical’. We no longer jump to explain everything first as a result of God. We see tree of divisions, and named parts and then look at how the language flows. And the, we, each of us measures the flows and, using our own words and mathematical descriptions, to compare and examine the divisions.

Then within these divisions language appears to hold the world as the paths between the now named ‘objects’ can be traced. From here we can then make theories, connections, and predictions that can be tested. This gives natural philosophers such as Newton new ways of speaking and writing about what can now be named and observed. It isa powerful method, and from it we gain finer language, finer measurements and, importantly, the ability to use them to build finer tools.

The Magic of Connecting Things With a Line of Invisibility
Yet if e look closely now at our words and the path they have taken we can begin to see, an unusual joining has taken place:

The named objects are taken from observations made within a world of extent. And then the mathematical relations are developed through objects from which extent has been deliberately removed.

When the two are joined, the calculation can appear exact even though every observation through which it is connected with the world remains finite.

Newton’s Mathematical World
Newton’s Principia, was first published in 1687, with the support of Edmund Halley. And famously is used the language of gravity and motion to draw the motions of bodies together within a new mathematical structure. These words and language are now firmly a part of our worldwide culture.

Newton’s words arrived with the authority of a book, the Royal Society, and the King They helped build a new language of of natural philosophy that was bound deeply to that of division and naming. Newton’s work is notably one of the most significant achievement academic to date by any standard. And it was made despite his playground, of alchemy, religion, and job as of the Royal Mint. Gravity is what we know Newton for - as an icon of division.

Newton’s enabled a person to follow the method and calculate a trajectory without needing to know what gravity ‘was’. What mattered was that the observed world of divisions permitted the relations, words, and symbols to hold sufficiently well.

This was the source and power of his work - it functioned against the measurements that could be made. A mathematical relation could be made seen across observations which had previously appeared disparate. Importantly, measured motions on Earth and in the heavens could be placed within the same symbolic framework. Predictions could be compared with later observations, and the framework could be extended, corrected and used again. Truly, language and the mathematical symbols used had shown it could be a very useful tool indeed.

Remembering the Naming
The words here, you may have noticed, become a story. And in this story the difficulty begins when the success of the sentences lets us forget the methods that made them work.

The equations work, and as a result they acquire an authority of their own. However, the pathway through which they were built becomes less visible: the words of Recorde, the divisions in language, the measurements, the instruments and the Euclidean instruction to use points and lines while removing the extent of every drawn point and line. The declaration of the infinity simple being fit for purpose that was simply declared by Wilkins, another member of the Royal Society. Nobody argued or voted for the symbol of infinity or asked if it was reasonable for lines and points to have no extent. There was no vote, there was small group of people and they placed us upon the path we now inhabit.

We also must remember that Newton was clear that his model was not the world - but it became the world. It set the path of how we think and how we do ‘mathematics’. As mathematics is not a ‘thing’ is is something we do, a specific path we take.

When the Words Become the World
There is no simple point at which a word becomes the world and we forget the path and the word’s own story. The change occurs through use. A term is repeated, a calculation succeeds, an instrument is built, a measurement is made and a prediction is confirmed. Each success adds weight to the symbolic pathway. Eventually later we follow the path without consideration for the past, the uncertainty, and the doubts that follow the word.

From here, the noun becomes a semantic entity which stands alone with the authority no longer insight. Yet around the name — the noun, remains a wider lattice of supporting trajectories: earlier observations, acts of registration, agreements about admissible use, translations, diagrams, instruments and institutions. Hidden from view these trajectories still hold the word and enable the word to function. But those trajectories are still there within the wider web of language. they hold the word in place.

And here the same story applies to a mathematical object. A point appears self-sufficient once its definition has been learned. Yet, its use still depend upon marks with extent, relations between marks and a reader who knows how to connect the symbols together from instructions that may be elsewhere and are not in plain sight.

Tiny Gods
Slowly, and surely, when the supporting pathways disappear from sight, we begin to speak as though bodies, forces, points and lines possess exactly the separations given to them by the language. We replace one God with thousands of tiny gods, each noun apparently carrying its own independent being.

Opposing Forces
History is a story of winners. the winners write the History. Especially when they hold the authority. Yet, the world of dinsision did not go unchallenged. It’s just the challengers names are hidden by the weight of years. The losers words perhaps lose a clarity of representation or are outright misrepresented. Politics touches and human divisions touch not only things but people, influence and the authority that enables a path to be continued. The challengers are assigned as lunatics, fools, or perhaps zealots. They measured a different world and used a different language. A language that was incompatible with that of division - it recognised the dilemma.

The Rebels
In the seventeenth century not everyone was comfortable with the movement towards division, or with the idea that we could keep splitting words into new words and somehow expect them to hold something beyond the words.

Many were simply unhappy that “God” was seemingly being left out of the picture. Others asked questions about how far the new mathematical and mechanical divisions could keep going. How could a word touch that which the word hoped to capture?

The European Connections
A story crosses can cross continents and not just time. The ideas of how the world was contained in language were deeply contested across Europe. New accounts of logic were being created, including what became known as the Port-Royal Logic.

When we look back, we find that the old books surrounded by correspondence from the great thinkers of the time. Many had their own helpers to enable their endeavours. They employed writers and secretaries, much as we now use AIs; they dictated notes, discussed ideas and sent hundreds of letters. They wrote books, just as we are writing now, and they argued — a lot.

Language was battlefield between thinkers, religion and the state. They often argued about God and where God should sit in the frame of language. They argued about what could constitute a ‘thing’. They argued about vacuum, matter, motion and causation, just as we argue now. They created schools of thought. And slowly, language stabilised across the centuries until many thoughts began to flow in something like the way we think today.

Blaise Pascal
There were a few outstanding thinkers of the seventeenth century whose words have been allowed to travel particularly strongly into our own time. Their works remain valued, their names remembered, and their contributions continue to form part of the language we use. One of these was Blaise Pascal—the Pascal after whom our modern unit of pressure is now named.

Like many thinkers of his period, Pascal concerned himself with some of the deepest questions of both religion and nature. He experimented with the vacuum, worked extensively with mathematics and calculation, and contributed directly to the mathematical developments of his age. Yet he was also deeply spiritual and committed to the idea of God. For Pascal these were not necessarily separate worlds. Mathematics, nature, language and religion met at the edges of what a human being could know.

Perhaps nowhere is this clearer than in his fascination with infinity. Pascal looked in both directions. Looking outward, he saw the apparently limitless scale of the heavens and the vastness of the universe. Looking inward, towards ever smaller divisions, he found another apparent infinity: a continuing descent into smaller and smaller parts. The human being seemed to stand somewhere between these two abysses—the infinitely large and the infinitely small—able to recognise both, yet unable to reach the end of either.

Much of Pascal’s thinking survives through fragments collected after his death as the Pensées. Reading them, we encounter a person repeatedly pushing words towards their limits. He knew the extraordinary power of calculation because he had practised it himself. He knew what reason and mathematics could accomplish. His concern was not that reason, logic or calculation were useless. Rather, it seems to me that he was concerned that we might mistake them for the whole of understanding.

Here Pascal begins to touch the central problem of our story. Mathematics can give us a symbol for infinity. We can write the sign, place it into an equation, and construct rules through which it can be manipulated. But having a symbol for infinity is not the same as encountering infinity. The word and the mathematical mark allow us to point towards something whose completion we can never measure.

Pascal therefore found himself standing at an edge of language. Human reason could travel an extraordinary distance, but perhaps one of its final acts was to recognise that there were things beyond the pathway it could construct. His famous distinction between the reasons of the mind and the “reasons of the heart” was not necessarily a rejection of reason. It was an acknowledgement that calculation did not exhaust human experience.

And perhaps this is why Pascal matters so much to our story. He lived within the very period in which mathematical language was becoming increasingly powerful, yet he did not simply surrender his entire world to that language. He could calculate, experiment and measure while still asking what remained beyond the calculation.

When Pascal looked towards the infinitely large and the infinitely small, he encountered something peculiar. The symbols could continue. The words could continue. The divisions could continue. But the person doing the dividing remained finite. Perhaps, then, infinity marks another boundary between the symbol and that towards which the symbol points. We can write infinity. We can speak of infinity. We can construct mathematical pathways that include infinity. But we cannot stand at its end and measure it. Pascal had reached the edge of the word.

Robert Greene
It’s not possible to discuss all the dissenters, or all those who opposed the emerging Newtonian viewpoint, and the story presented here is, after all, just a story for your ear. However, it is perhaps worth telling the tale of a fellow academic who was something of an outsider. And, like many outsiders, he held a different view of the world and sensed that language could, and perhaps should, take a different path.

In 1712, Robert Greene at Cambridge published his own Principles of Natural Philosophy. Unlike Newton’s Principia, this is a book you are unlikely to have heard of. There were no future editions. It was not supported by the Royal Society, and it did not carry the authority of the King. Greene is now almost unknown, and certainly a far less familiar figure than Newton, but his relative obscurity is itself part of our story.

Greene questioned the adequacy of the dominant partitions of matter and space. He noticed some of the same difficulties that Blaise Pascal could see and tried to recover something that the new mathematical organisation of the world appeared to leave unresolved.

It was an ancient problem. Continually dividing the world into matter and vacuum did not finally give us the world. The words matter and vacuum could not themselves touch it. Matter could be divided again, and then divided once more. Words could divide and name these different parts, but the greater named thing remained stubbornly present. A gold bar could be described as matter and space, divided into smaller parts and then smaller parts again, but if the bar fell upon your foot it could still fracture the bone.

The words were not touching the observed world.

This is an observation that continues to haunt modern physics. When we model something such as a lead atom, we find a structure that appears overwhelmingly to consist of empty space. Yet collect enough lead atoms into a bar, drop that bar, and it will fall to the floor—and, if your foot happens to be underneath it, the distinction between matter and empty space will offer very little comfort.

Greene did not stop the trajectory of Newton, and perhaps that is a good thing. But his presence tells us that the trajectory was never uncontested. The apparent certainty of the things we inherit afterwards conceals the arguments that surrounded their formation.

And now here we are, centuries later, inhabiting our own language. We largely inhabit the pathway of Newton. The pathways of Greene, and the concerns that travelled along them, have become much harder to see. They were not necessarily disproved; many were simply left behind. And a pathway that is no longer travelled can become almost impossible to walk.

William Blake
Telling any story involves choosing what to include and what to leave behind. And yet a story wants to move on. Any good story has a beginning, a middle and an end. Before we come to the end, it is perhaps worth going back to the beginning for just a moment, to remember where we started.

At the very beginning we placed a picture of Newton. Not Newton as a wise old man, but as an almost Greek, statuesque figure, naked and seated upon a rock. This was a print by William Blake. Blake’s particular skill was to communicate not only with words, but through symbols and images. The picture was being used to tell a story, and you, the reader, were asked to find the story within the picture.

William Blake inhabited the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries and lived with the later consequences of this changing world. The work of Newton and the institutions of the Royal Society had become increasingly established. Ideas and language were becoming more rigid, and Blake was deeply concerned by what he saw. It seemed that the world was tightening. Language itself appeared to be losing a sense of that wider world which its divisions could never quite touch.

Blake was a poet, painter, engraver and printer: a maker of images and words who understood their material construction. In his writing and pictures we find an objection not simply to Newton as a man, nor to every use of reason, but to the narrowing of perception when one symbolic pathway comes to claim the whole field of vision. He felt the doors of perception being closed. Language could stifle as well as reveal, while never-ending division could build walls within the greater world of experience.

The story of Blake is therefore not the story of another formal system. His was a story of imagination and of the human capacity to encounter, create and particularise a world that no general diagram could ever exhaust.

Importantly, Blake made images as well as arguments. He left us material artefacts that we can still encounter with our own senses. It seems to me that he understood that an image could hold relations simultaneously which a sequence of declarative sentences might struggle to hold at all.

We will not yet return fully to his picture, but for now it is enough to notice something rather curious. Within Blake’s picture the compass has extent. Newton’s hand has extent. His body has extent. The material upon which he draws has extent. The rock beneath him has extent. And yet Newton’s attention is directed towards a geometry drawn upon a flat scroll, a geometry ultimately founded upon points without parts and lines without breadth. Blake places the finite body of the mathematician looking down towards his construction, while the greater natural world remains around him. For the moment, we can leave him there.

Where We Have Arrived
The two pathways have now joined. The world has been partitioned into named objects, and the relations between those objects can be treated through a mathematics that has removed the extent and uncertainty of its marks. Newton brings these inheritances together within a structure of extraordinary power.

The compass of division is not evil, and geometry is not false. The question is what happens when the success of the construction hides the construction itself. Pascal, Greene and Blake remind us that the pathway was never without alternatives and never beyond dispute.

The authority once carried principally by God has not vanished. It has moved and fractured: into institutions, methods, published texts, measurements and symbols. We can now follow that authority into mathematics itself.

Part V — When the Symbol Became the Measure
The Authority of the Symbol
By the time we reach Locke, Newton and the generations that followed, methods of partition and abstraction had become extraordinarily successful. The mind could be described through faculties and operations. The world could be described through bodies, forces and measurable relations. Knowledge could be arranged into disciplines, and each discipline could establish its own admissible questions.

This was not simply Ramus repeated. Too much had happened, and too many different people and traditions had intervened. Yet I think we can still follow a family resemblance. A complex field is divided; the divisions are named; relations are established between the names; and the resulting symbolic structure becomes a means by which later observations are admitted or rejected.

The authority once carried by ‘God’ had not disappeared so much as fractured and moved. Some of it remained in religion. Some passed into the state, the university, the scientific society and the published paper. Some became attached to number itself. A measured result could appear to speak without a speaker, even though an instrument, a method, a calibration, a decision and a humanly constructed chain of symbols always stood behind it. However Recorde didn’t just invent a symbol. He introduced it and placing it in his book he said :

“This is what equality looks like. From now on, when you see these two parallel lines, you will know: the thing on the left is exactly the thing on the right.”

And if we look hard enough we can see that he didn’t discover equality. He ordained its representation. And because it was printed in a book, that ordination could travel and taught. It could be repeated, to the point that it could become natural. So natural that we forget it was ever justa sequence of words. This is the power of a book. It turns a decision into a fact.

A Dispute About Mathematics
The same tension appears again, in a different form, during the controversy between L. E. J. Brouwer and David Hilbert in the early twentieth century. By then mathematics had developed symbolic structures of a reach that Ramus could not have imagined. Questions of infinity, formal proof and the foundations of mathematics were no longer confined to simple divisions on a printed page, yet the question of authority remained.

Hilbert sought to secure mathematics through a formal programme. He wished to preserve the powerful ideal structures of modern mathematics while establishing their consistency through trusted finitary means. Brouwer took a different path. For him, mathematics was not founded in a completed external world of symbols. It arose through human mathematical construction, grounded in an intuition that preceded its linguistic expression.

Their disagreement was technical, philosophical and institutional. It also became personal. In 1928, Hilbert removed Brouwer from the editorial board of Mathematische Annalen. Brouwer was deeply wounded and withdrew substantially from public mathematical work for a period. Three years later, Gödel’s incompleteness results exposed limits within the hope that a sufficiently powerful formal system could establish all the security Hilbert’s programme sought.

It would be too easy to turn this into a story in which Brouwer was simply right and Hilbert wrong. That would repeat the very partition we are examining. Hilbert was responding to genuine problems, and formal mathematics has produced results of enormous value. Brouwer was also not offering an uncomplicated path that everyone else refused through blindness. What matters for our story is that one approach acquired greater institutional continuity while another became more marginal.

The symbols did not win by standing up and defeating intuition. People, universities, journals, curricula and practical successes carried one pathway more readily than another. Again, the movement of knowledge depended upon its material and institutional means of reproduction.

Euclid’s point had no part, but it continued to require a mark. Hilbert’s formal symbols could be operated upon according to rules, but they continued to require finite inscriptions, readers and acts of construction. Brouwer’s objection reopens the pathway that formal presentation tends to compress: the mathematical object does not arrive without an activity through which it is made available.

When the Measure Became a Definition
For most of my professional life, measurement was never an abstract matter. An instrument had a history. It had limits, calibration records, tolerances and conditions of use. A number did not simply appear; it emerged from an interaction between an object, an instrument and a chain of decisions about what would count as a registration.

That experience is part of why the history of the metre interests me. From 1889, the International Prototype Metre was a platinum-iridium bar. It was a carefully constructed and protected object against which other standards could be related. It had extent. It could be located, inspected and compared, although every comparison carried uncertainty.

In 1960, the metre was redefined through a specified wavelength associated with krypton-86. In 1983, it was redefined as the distance travelled by light in vacuum during a stated fraction of a second. Each change was made for intelligible reasons: greater reproducibility, stability and precision. Yet something philosophically interesting occurred. The unit moved from a particular material artefact towards a relation held in a system of definitions.

The measured value of the speed of light was set within the definition at exactly 299,792,458 metres per second. The number was placed in stone. It no longer operated within that definition as a measured estimate awaiting refinement. Any uncertainty that had belonged to the preceding measurement history was not improved by the act of setting the number; it was moved outside the number and into the realisation of the metre, the second and the surrounding measurement system.

The modern metre has not ceased to be useful, and laboratories do not create length merely by writing a number. Instruments must still realise the definition through physical interactions. Nevertheless, it seems to me that the symbolic account can hide that material work. The definition appears exact while every act through which we encounter it remains finite and uncertain.

The older bar displayed its vulnerability. It could age, change temperature or acquire damage. Those were practical difficulties, but they were also reminders that the standard belonged to the measured world. The newer definition places exactness in the symbols and leaves uncertainty to the act of realisation. In doing so, perhaps it encourages us to treat uncertainty as an imperfection of our access rather than as part of what measurement is.

This is one place where the Great Parting becomes visible to me. The symbol no longer merely records the measured object; the symbolic relation defines the unit through which objects will be measured. The pathway has turned back upon the world.

Euclid asked us to disregard the breadth of the drawn line in order to enter an exact geometrical relation. The modern definition asks us to accept an exact numerical relation while every practical metre continues to be registered through interactions with finite extent. The cases are not identical, but perhaps they share a family resemblance: exactness resides within the rule, while uncertainty resides in the material act through which the rule is encountered.

Lorenz and the Returning Uncertainty
In 1961, Edward Lorenz was working with a computer model of weather. He restarted a calculation using values printed to fewer decimal places than those stored inside the machine. The difference appeared tiny. Yet as the model continued, its later trajectory moved away from the earlier one until the two no longer resembled each other.

The story has become famous as part of what we now call sensitive dependence on initial conditions. Its importance here is not that Lorenz proved all prediction impossible. He did something subtler. He showed that, even within a deterministic symbolic model, a difference small enough to be discarded by one registration could become decisive later in the trajectory.

The omitted digits had not looked important. The compression appeared harmless. But the system carried the difference forward and enlarged it. What had been removed from the printed number returned as a different future.

For me, Lorenz does not destroy reason or measurement. He restores humility to them. A model can be useful within a particular horizon without being a complete possession of the future. A small uncertainty is not always a small matter, and a symbol that looks sufficiently precise at one stage may not preserve everything required by the trajectory that follows.

The deeper lesson is that uncertainty cannot always be eliminated by adding more symbols. At some point every observation must be registered with finite resolution. Every computational sequence begins from values that have already passed through selection and representation. The calculation may be exact according to its rules while the relationship between those rules and the measured world remains open.

Where We Have Arrived
The symbolic pathway has turned back upon the world. What began as a method for carrying selected relations now participates in defining the units through which later observations become admissible. Exactness belongs to the declared relation; uncertainty has been relocated into the material acts required to realise it.

Brouwer reminds us that mathematics is never encountered without construction. The metre reminds us that definitions are never realised without interaction. Lorenz reminds us that what is removed from one registration may return later in the trajectory.

The question is no longer whether symbols are useful. Their usefulness is beyond reasonable dispute. The question is whether their success has brought us to mistake finite symbolic structures for the whole field from which they were made.

Part VI — The Threshold and the Return
The Threshold
Thomas Kuhn later gave us a language for thinking about the way scientific frameworks persist and change. A paradigm does not fall whenever a troublesome observation appears. An anomaly can be ignored, accommodated or treated as a problem for later work. Only when the supporting framework can no longer carry the growing difficulty does a different pathway become possible.

Perhaps we are approaching such a threshold, although I do not claim to know. Across physics, cosmology, mathematics, economics and the study of complex systems, extraordinarily powerful symbolic structures sit beside unresolved questions about measurement, interpretation and extent. We continue to construct finer instruments and larger calculations, but the act of registration has not disappeared. Somewhere, an interaction still has to become a symbol.

The temptation is to respond by announcing another complete system. I do not want to do that here. This story is not a manifesto and it is not a replacement priesthood. It is an inquiry into how we arrived at the questions we are now able—and sometimes unable—to ask.

Partition is not the enemy. Without distinction, there can be no word, no measurement and no story. Abstraction is not the enemy either. It allows a finite symbolic arrangement to travel beyond the immediate event that produced it. The problem begins when we forget the cost: the neighbouring pathways removed by the partition, the extent removed from the geometrical object, the uncertainty excluded from the number, and the history compressed out of the surviving statement.

Perhaps a different approach would not abandon reason but resituate it. It would recognise that measurements are finite registrations rather than disembodied truths. It would retain uncertainty and provenance instead of treating them as inconvenient additions. It would allow intuition, participation and lived experience to stand beside formal analysis without forcing one to erase the other.

Most of all, it would leave room for the reader. If the author closes every relation and supplies every conclusion, the reader can only accept or reject the finished structure. If enough of the provenance is shown, but some of the pathways remain open, meaning can be reconstructed through the reader’s own history. The reader then becomes part of the authorship of the work.

That is why I began by telling you something about myself. It was not a claim that my life makes the story true. It was an attempt to show you where these words came from, so that you could bring your own provenance to meet them.

Returning to Newton
We can now return to the picture with which we began.

Newton is still seated there. His body is folded forward and his attention is directed towards the geometrical construction beneath his compass. Around him is a richly textured world which does not share the clean order of the diagram. His body belongs to that surrounding world, yet his gaze is concentrated upon the marks he is making.

The compass is not evil, and geometry is not false. The concentration is real, and the symbolic construction is powerful. Blake does not need to make Newton foolish for the image to carry a tension. The picture can hold both the extraordinary reach of geometrical reason and the world that remains outside its immediate field of attention.

Perhaps the nakedness matters. Newton has not escaped the material world; he is physically continuous with it. Perhaps the posture matters too. His whole body is participating in the act of abstraction, even as the abstraction seems to direct him away from the body and towards the diagram. Perhaps the rock, the colour and the surrounding textures are not a background at all. They may be the greater part of the picture.

Now Euclid is also present. He is not painted as another figure beside Newton, but his instructions lie within the compass and the construction. The visible points have extent, but Newton reads them as though they have no parts. The visible lines have breadth, but Newton follows the relations of breadthless length. The material picture shows us a body using an instrument with extent to enter a geometry from which extent has been removed.

Ramus is present too, although he is not visible. The field has been divided, the parts have been named, and attention has been directed along an admissible pathway. Behind the image stand the printer, translator, mathematician, natural philosopher, institution and reader. Blake’s single picture gathers a history which no single figure within it could contain.

I do not want to tell you what Blake’s image finally means. That would replace the picture with another small book of instructions. Nor do I claim that Blake intended every pathway we have followed from Ramus through Euclid, Newton, Greene, Brouwer, Hilbert, the metre and Lorenz. An image can carry relations beyond the declared intention of the person who made it, just as a printed method can travel beyond the life of its author.

What I can say is that the image now holds something of this story for me. The compass opens a measured relation. The marks give that relation a symbolic form. Newton’s attention gives the symbols direction. His body provides extent. The surrounding world remains present, irregular and not exhausted by the construction on the page.

At the beginning, I asked what you saw. The image itself has not changed during the time it has taken us to return. The compass has not moved. The marks remain where they were, and Newton is still bent over them. But you have now travelled through a story about words, printing, authority, partition, Euclid’s removal of extent, measurement and uncertainty.

So I shall not give you a final explanation. I shall only place the image before you once more and ask the same question in a slightly different way.

What do you see now?

Part VII — The Continuum as a Commitment
We have travelled from the little book beside me to the compass in Blake’s image, through the divisions of Ramus, the abstraction of Euclid, the measurements of Newton, the unheeded questions of Greene, and the returning uncertainty of Lorenz. I have not told you that one pathway was right and another wrong. I have tried only to show you the pathways themselves, their costs, their exclusions and their power.

But there is a deeper question that has been waiting for us all along, and perhaps you have already felt it pressing at the edges of the words.

What is the continuum?

The word has appeared many times in our story. The continuum is what we imagine lies beyond our discrete steps. It is the real world to which our measurements point. It is the flow beneath the partitions, the territory beneath the map. It is what we have been losing, forgetting, and trying to recover.

And yet—what if the continuum itself is a symbol?

We have never measured a continuum. We have never touched one. We have never seen one. Every instrument gives us discrete readings. Every observation is a finite registration. Every number is a step, and every step is a choice. The continuum is never given to us directly. It is always inferred, always projected, always imagined.

The continuum may or may not be “real.” But that is not the point. The point is that we cannot know it directly. Whatever lies beyond our symbols is unknowable—not because it is hidden, but because the act of knowing is itself an act of symbol-making. To know something is to bring it into language, into number, into relation. The moment we try to touch the continuum, we have already divided it, named it, stepped within it.

The continuum is not a discovery. It is a commitment—an act of faith that there is something beyond our measurements that holds them together. It is a guess about what lies beyond the limits of our registration. And it is an act of imagination: the capacity to project unity onto a world that we have only ever encountered in fragments.

This is not a failure of measurement. It is the condition under which measurement is possible. Without the commitment to a continuum, the discrete steps would not hang together. They would be isolated, meaningless, unconnected. The continuum is the glue that makes our symbols cohere. But the glue is not given to us by the world; it is given by us, through our imagination.

Now we can see the history of our story differently. Ramus did not discover a method of division; he chose one. Euclid did not uncover a realm of ideal objects; he invited us to remove extent from the marks we made. Newton did not find laws already written into the heavens; he committed his imagination to a mathematical world, and it worked. Cardano committed his imagination to a world of experiment and observation. Scaliger committed his to a world of textual authority and logical order. Greene committed his to a world that could not be exhausted by either.

None of them was simply “right” or “wrong.” They were making different commitments—different guesses about what lies beyond the symbols.

And that is why the game is far from over. The choices are never final. We can always choose differently. We can always make different guesses. We can always commit our imagination to a different arrangement of symbols. The pathways that were closed are not destroyed; they are sleeping. The questions that were silenced are not answered; they are waiting. And as long as there are readers willing to reactivate those pathways, the game continues.

Blake’s Newton now appears in a different light. He is not discovering a pre-existing order. He is building one, with compass and ink, with imagination and commitment. The point without parts and the breadthless line are not features of the world; they are agreements, instructions, acts of collective imagining. And they are powerful—so powerful that we have come to treat them as though they were the world itself.

But we know now that they are not. The continuum is a symbol. It is a guess. It is a commitment of the imagination. And every commitment is also a choice.

The question is not whether your symbols are true. The question is whether you remember that they are symbols, and whether you are willing to choose them again, with full knowledge of what they carry and what they leave behind.

So I shall not tell you what to believe. I have no authority to do that. I am only a storyteller, following the words as they flow into each other, showing you the pathways as I have found them.

But I will leave you with the question that has been waiting for us all along:

What are you willing to commit your imagination to?

The answer is not a symbol. The answer is the practice of symbolising, with full knowledge that every symbol is finite, every registration is a choice, and every choice is yours to make again.





I have beside me a copy of Greene's Principles of Natural Philosophy (1712). It cost me £150. Its title page is missing. Its authority is incomplete. A first edition of Newton's Principia would cost me more than £150,000. The difference is not merely about rarity. It is about which books we have chosen to carry forward, which pathways we have preserved, and which we have allowed to decay. Greene's book is a reminder that the story of science is not a simple accumulation of truth. It is a selection—a series of decisions about which voices should be heard and which should be silenced.
