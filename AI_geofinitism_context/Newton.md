# Newton Beneath the Sea: Light, Gravity, and the Conditions of Observation

There is something deeply unsettling in William Blake’s image of Newton. At first glance, the scene appears readily available to us. Newton sits upon a rock, or perhaps a reef, at what seems to be the bottom of the sea. He is young, powerful and intensely absorbed. Before him is a scroll, and upon the scroll is a geometrical construction that he measures with a pair of dividers. Around him is darkness, colour, texture and organic form, yet his whole attention has been drawn into the small pale surface held between his hands.

The familiar interpretation is that Blake is opposing imagination to reason, or perhaps art to science. There is clearly something of that in the image, but for me it does not reach the more fundamental problem Blake has placed before us. Newton is not merely failing to notice the living world around him. He is attempting to divide and describe a world whose conditions of observation have already been removed from the description. The image is not simply about what Newton sees. It is about what must happen before Newton can see anything at all.

This brings light to the centre of the picture, but not as a metaphor for knowledge or spiritual illumination. Without light—whatever light ultimately is or is not—there can be no visual observation of Newton, the rock, the scroll or the line. More importantly, there can be no visual observation of the relationship we subsequently call gravity. Newton’s foot can appear locked into the rock only because light makes the foot, rock and relation between them distinguishable. His weight, posture and downward placement can enter the image only because a difference has become available for registration.

Gravity is therefore not first observed independently and then illuminated by light so that we may inspect it. The observation through which gravity becomes available already depends upon light. We see a body fall, a pendulum move, an orbit change or a measuring instrument produce a different state. Each case requires a distinction, a registration of that distinction and some retention against which the new state can be compared. Without this pathway there is no measurement of gravity, or of anything else for that matter.

This does not yet mean that gravity *is* light, nor that the equations used for gravitational and electromagnetic descriptions can simply be exchanged. The claim is more careful, but perhaps also more disruptive. Gravity has never been empirically established as independent of light because every completed observation of gravity already contains the means by which a difference was disclosed, registered and retained. We may separate gravity from light within a later symbolic construction, but the separation does not occur in the measurement event itself. It occurs upon the scroll.

## Before gravity can be named

It seems to me that the ordinary grammar of science encourages us to begin too late. We begin with gravity as a noun, as though it were already present as a distinct object waiting to be measured. We then add an observer, instrument and signal, treating these as external aids used to discover the pre-existing entity. By the time the measurement is written down, the sequence appears simple: gravity produces an effect, the instrument detects it, and the observer records the result.

Yet if we unfold that sequence, the apparent simplicity begins to disappear. A body occupies one registered position and is later registered in another. A detector changes state. A marker moves across a scale. An interference pattern shifts. An electrical value crosses a threshold and becomes a number. The word *gravity* does not occur at the first boundary of any of these events. It appears after the changes have been selected, retained, compared and placed within an inherited explanatory framework.

In the most direct human observation, light enables the differentiated positions to become visible. In an instrumented observation, the route may be longer: a mechanical displacement may become an electrical change, the electrical change may be digitised, and the resulting symbol may appear upon a screen or printed surface. Even then, the measurement is not complete as human knowledge until the difference has become accessible and retained. The instrument does not remove the problem of observation; it extends and reorganises the trajectory through which observation becomes possible.

What, then, do I mean by light? I do not want to decide at the beginning that light is a photon, wave, field, corpuscle or ray. Each of those words belongs to a developed symbolic framework and carries a history of instruments, measurements and theoretical choices. At the lowest order, light is implicated in the interaction through which a difference becomes available for registration. A ray may preserve the direction of that interaction; a photon may act as an accounting unit; a wave may organise regularities across a trajectory. None of these need be mistaken for an unmediated statement of what light finally is.

Perhaps we can say that light is not first encountered as an independent entity but as a stabilised distinction. A source, path, receiver, detector condition and retained comparison participate in the event. Darkness is not simply nothing; it supplies a differentiated condition against which another condition can be registered. A detector count is meaningful only against a background, a pixel value against another pixel value, and an illuminated mark against the surface upon which it appears.

The same requirement applies to gravity. Gravity is not registered without a directed signal, a retained displacement or some other measurable change. Light is not registered without a materially positioned source and receiver, a detector state and memory. What we later divide into gravity, ray and memory may therefore be analytical unfoldings of one completed measurement event rather than three independently assembled things.

For me, the provisional identity is better held as a compound:

\[
\lvert G,\,\mathrm{Ray},\,\mathrm{Memory}\rangle,
\]

where the notation does not assert that gravity and light are the same substance. It reminds us that the gravity-associated configuration, the directed trajectory through which a difference becomes available, and the retained registration are inseparable within the completed measurement. If we remove the ray or other distinction-bearing interaction, there is no observed change. If we remove memory, there is no comparison. If we remove the material placement and relation that we later gather beneath gravity, there is no event to distinguish.

The scientific noun is produced only after this compound has been compressed. The measurement is unfolded in the world and through the instrument, but the resulting language folds it into a shorter symbolic object. Once the word *gravity* has become stable, it can travel through equations and sentences without carrying the light, detector, memory, uncertainty and provenance that made its formation possible. The compression is useful, but it creates the appearance of independence.

## Pascal and the prepared world

Blaise Pascal is often placed within the history of mathematics, mechanics, probability and religion as though these were separable regions of his work. Yet it seems to me that Pascal was already examining the point at which a successful method begins to exceed the conditions under which it became successful. His distinction between the geometric mind and the finer or intuitive mind was not a rejection of geometry. Pascal knew the power of geometry too well for that. It was a recognition that geometric clarity requires a prepared world.

Before a proof can proceed, the terms must be defined, the admissible operations agreed and the field reduced sufficiently for a deductive sequence to be followed. This works extraordinarily well when the problem has been made suitable for it. But what of the acts through which the terms were first established? What of the measurements, judgements and relations that do not arrive already partitioned? The geometric method can proceed only after its starting points have been stabilised, yet it cannot always establish those starting points by its own method.

Something has already happened before the proof begins. A material difference has been registered, converted into a finite mark and placed within a shared language. The proof inherits that mark as though the primary difficulty has been resolved. It can then manipulate the symbol without repeatedly reconstructing the event from which the symbol arose.

For me, this is close to the Greene–Pascal limit. Language can partition, stabilise and communicate, but it cannot carry the whole trajectory from which its symbols have been drawn. A word does not arrive with every earlier use, every instrument that supported it, every uncertainty that accompanied it or every alternative distinction that might have been made. Its usefulness depends upon a great deal of this structure remaining outside the immediate sentence.

The danger is not that reason fails. It is that a particular form of reason may become so successful within its prepared field that the preparation itself disappears from view. The compression product is then mistaken for the source manifold. What began as a method for handling registered distinctions becomes an account of what the world contains.

## Robert Greene and the divided world

Robert Greene enters this trajectory from a different and now largely forgotten position. Writing in Cambridge during the period in which Newtonian natural philosophy was acquiring its authority, Greene challenged the division of the world into passive matter, void and mechanically described relation. His language remained theological, and that must be allowed to remain part of his world rather than being cleaned away to make him resemble a modern critic.

For Greene, the universe was connected because its origin and continuation were held within divine creation. Yet even here a fascinating difficulty appears. If God speaks and the world begins, the words by which human beings describe that event belong to the created world, not to eternity. Language must therefore turn back upon itself. It attempts to name the condition from which naming arose while remaining unable to leave the world in which its own marks are made.

Perhaps Greene understood that the grammar of natural philosophy always carries a small bubble of indeterminacy. The word cannot be placed directly over the world with no remainder. Matter and vacuum may be useful distinctions, but dividing the world between them does not reconstruct the world. We may divide matter again into smaller forms, and divide those forms once more, but the repeated operation of division gives us a finer symbolic account produced by the same grammar. It does not give us a position outside the world from which the division can be certified as complete.

This is where Greene’s dispute with the emerging Newtonian settlement becomes more than an old disagreement over forces, matter and theology. Newton’s framework offered an immensely effective way of arranging observations and making calculations. Greene’s question, as I now read him, was whether the success of that arrangement justified the ontology that seemed to follow from it. Did the world contain the nouns that the method required, already separated and waiting to be found? Or had the method produced stable semantic entities and then returned them to the world as its fundamental furniture?

Greene did not provide a closed answer, and neither do I think we should manufacture one for him. His expansive and contractive forces carried their own language, assumptions and theological commitments. Yet a challenge can disclose a limit without supplying the final system beyond it. Indeed, the absence of a closed replacement may be precisely why such a challenge is easy to ignore. An established framework can demand an alternative expressed according to the admissibility rules of the framework being questioned, and the original partition then appears to defend itself.

The historical silence around Greene is therefore interesting. Perhaps his work was wrong in many particulars. Perhaps it was excessive or attached to a metaphysical structure already losing authority. But silence is not a demonstration. It is also one way in which a symbolic community protects its working coherence. A trajectory that is not answered cannot easily become part of the recognised debate, and over time its absence begins to look like proof that no debate existed.

## Blake places the problem before us

Blake gives this difficulty a body. His Newton is not the elderly, bewigged figure carrying institutional authority, but a young man of extraordinary strength. The youth seems important to me. This is not a picture of incapacity. Newton is capable, disciplined and completely committed to his labour. Yet there is also something of the young man’s concentration in him: the immediate operation has become the whole field, while the conditions that make the operation possible remain outside his gaze.

The scene is usually described as underwater because the formations around Newton resemble coral, sponge and the living growth of a reef. The darkness provides no horizon and no obvious direction. There is no sky, sun or distant marker by which the scene can be placed. Newton therefore appears within a world without a visible global reference frame, but he has constructed a local one. The scroll supplies a bounded surface, the lines provide direction, and the dividers preserve an interval. Within this small prepared territory, the work can proceed.

The asymmetry is remarkable. The geometrical construction occupies very little of the whole image, yet it occupies almost all of Newton’s attention. The surrounding field contains colour, curvature, depth, irregularity and the accumulated forms of growth, while the scroll carries only a few lines and relations. The small symbolic surface has become the operational world.

Yet none of this would be available without light. The reef is not simply a metaphorical nature that Newton ignores. It is part of the differentiated field against which Newton, his body and his actions can be registered. The darkness does not merely symbolise ignorance. It produces contrast. Newton’s illuminated body, the pale scroll and the darker surrounding surface create the finite distinctions through which the viewer can reconstruct the scene.

This makes the relation between gravity and light central rather than decorative. Gravity is presented through Newton’s placement, weight and bodily attachment to the rock. But we do not observe that attachment without light. The gravitational relation is disclosed through the visible differences between body, foot, rock and direction. Light is therefore not a second subject added beside gravity. It is within the observation by which gravity becomes present to us at all.

Blake’s image could not make gravity visible by painting gravity directly. He paints relations: a body bent towards the scroll, a foot apparently absorbed into the rock, weight carried by a surface and limbs directed by position. We gather those visible relations beneath the noun *gravity*. The painting quietly preserves the path from relation to name, while Newton’s diagram appears to begin after the name has already become available.

## The locked foot and the gravitational observation

One of Newton’s feet appears from beneath the body and rock, but the leg that would connect it to him is not available to the eye. Anatomically, it may simply be occluded. Symbolically, however, the effect is strange and powerful. We see the terminal object without seeing the trajectory that joins it to the body. The foot seems at once to belong to Newton and to have become part of the rock.

For me, Newton is locked into the surface upon which he sits. Gravity is not merely the subject of a calculation. It is enacted through his placement, posture and inability to turn. It fixes his direction towards his labour. He attempts to place gravity within a symbolic construction while Blake places Newton within the relation that the word *gravity* will later compress.

But the lock is visible only because the foot and rock are differentiated. Light is participating in the gravitational observation. Remove the illuminating distinction and the foot does not appear locked, free or attached; it does not appear at all. We cannot first extract gravity from this scene and only afterwards acknowledge the light that allows us to see it. The visible gravitational relation and the means of its disclosure arrive together.

The missing leg also carries the wider problem of provenance. A registered endpoint is present, but its connection has disappeared. Scientific language often behaves in the same way. The final quantity remains while the pathway of source, apparatus, transformation, threshold, uncertainty and comparison is removed from the working statement. Once the connection has been dropped, the named object appears to stand independently.

This may be what happened to gravity as a semantic entity. There are repeatable observations and useful mathematical relations. Bodies change position, instruments change state and predictions acquire remarkable practical power. Something has unquestionably been registered. But the word *gravity* gathers these trajectories together, compresses them and begins to behave as a separable noun. The success of the compression is then taken as evidence that the entity itself was encountered independently.

The painting shows why that inference may not be admissible. The foot cannot be separated from the light through which its placement is observed, the rock against which its position becomes meaningful, or the viewer’s retained knowledge of bodily anatomy. Gravity does not arrive as a solitary object. It arrives as a reconstructed relation within a completed observation.

## The body formed as G

Once Newton’s body is viewed in this way, another form begins to appear. His folded back, thigh, arms and inward-facing posture approximate the letter **G**. Perhaps this was not intended by Blake as a concealed code. But the reader is also an author, and once the form becomes visible it opens a productive trajectory. The **G** may carry geometry, gravity and the governing frame itself.

Newton does not merely use geometry; his body has been drawn into its shape. The opening of the bodily **G** faces the scroll. His gaze, face, arms, finger and dividers all direct us towards the same region. The posture has become a physical channel through which attention flows into the line.

This, for me, is one of Blake’s deepest observations. A representational system does not merely provide a passive description. Repeated use gives it direction, and that direction begins to act back upon the user. Newton’s body has become the sign of the system he appears to command. Gravity holds the **G** in place, light makes the **G** distinguishable, and geometry gives the **G** its operative direction.

The three cannot be cleanly separated within the visible event. Their separation occurs only when one trajectory is selected and the others are removed from the account. Blake allows us to see the compound before Newton’s symbolic surface divides it.

## The robe, the scroll and Euclid’s line

The pale surface upon which Newton works does not seem to begin simply as a scroll. It appears to descend from behind his shoulder as a flowing material, rather like a robe that he is not wearing, and then to become the flattened two-dimensional surface upon which he measures. There is no decisive boundary at which cloth ends and symbolic plane begins. A continuous folded material passes into a reduced surface.

This matters because the first act of compression has occurred before Newton uses the dividers. The world has already been prepared for geometry. The continuous surface has been flattened, cleaned and made capable of holding distinct marks. Newton begins his work downstream of that transformation and therefore does not see it as an operation. The plane arrives as though it were simply there.

Behind him lies inheritance. Greek geometry, writing, instruments, paper, accumulated practice and the long authority of the line all flow into Newton’s field of work without becoming part of the visible diagram. The rolled end of the material resembles the volute of an ancient column. It is at once scroll, architectural support and a reminder that the supposedly ideal plane remains a material object with thickness, curvature and extent.

Newton’s finger points towards a line. But what is this line? Euclid’s geometrical line has length without breadth, while the line before Newton must have breadth or Blake could not show it to us. It has pigment, a surface and a history of inscription. Light must interact with this finite mark for the line to be seen. The ideal line is produced only when these physical conditions are removed from consideration.

It seems to me that Blake brings the cost of the ink back into geometry. The scroll curls. The line has width. The instrument has materially formed points. Newton’s finger touches a surface. The body is held by the rock and disclosed by light. Everything excluded by the ideal construction remains visibly present around it.

Gravity may then be read as something like the geometer’s line. This does not mean that bodies do not fall or that no measurements have been made. It means that the apparently independent semantic entity has been formed through selection and removal. Relations are gathered, compressed and given a name; the name is then treated as though it were the source of those relations. The ideal is downstream of the registration, not the other way around.

The scroll is therefore where gravity can be separated from light. Upon the scroll, the registered relation is translated into marks and quantities. The light-dependent trajectory that made the observation possible no longer needs to be written. Gravity can then move through the symbolic system as though it had been measured alone. Blake’s picture surrounds the scroll with the very conditions that the scroll allows Newton to forget.

## Light is not merely illumination

It would be easy to return light to the familiar metaphor of knowledge. Newton is brightly illuminated while the surrounding sea is dark, and we might say that he possesses clarity but lacks breadth. This reading is not wrong, but it is insufficient. It makes light a literary device when Blake has made it a material condition of the visible work.

Without a light-associated interaction, Newton cannot see the line. Without the difference between line and scroll, the dividers have no visible positions to compare. Without a retained state, no interval can be preserved. The geometrical operation itself is therefore light-dependent, even before Newton uses it to describe gravity. Light enables the symbolic surface upon which light and gravity will later be treated as separate domains.

The source of illumination is absent from the picture, but its effect is everywhere. Newton’s body is modelled by changes in brightness. The scroll is distinguished from the surrounding field. The rock acquires texture. The line acquires width and direction. Light is absent as an object while present as the enabling interaction of the entire image.

Perhaps this is why the picture feels as though it contains more than a contrast between science and imagination. Newton is unable to place the condition of seeing inside the field he sees. The light through which the world is distinguished disappears into the transparency of successful observation. He can then direct his attention towards gravity as though gravity had announced itself independently.

This remains true when the eye is replaced by an instrument. A modern gravimeter, astronomical detector or interferometer does not encounter a label reading *gravity*. It produces a state change that must be transduced, compared and retained. LIGO, for example, does not receive a gravitational wave as a finished semantic object. It uses controlled light, interference, photodetection, digitisation, calibration, filtering, comparison and classification. The final phrase *gravitational wave* is the compressed endpoint of a long measurement trajectory in which light is not incidental.

The same pattern appears in simpler observations. A falling object must pass through distinguishable positions. A pendulum requires successive states and a means of timing them. An orbit must be reconstructed from registered positions. The measurements vary greatly, but none gives us gravity prior to the interaction through which change becomes detectable. We never remove light or its functional equivalent from the completed observation; we remove it from the final sentence.

## What kind of claim is being made?

At this point a distinction is necessary. To say that gravity cannot be empirically established as independent of light is not yet to say that gravity and light are ontologically identical. The first claim concerns measurement. The second would concern what the world finally is, and it would require another operational pathway capable of distinguishing identity from inseparability.

Within the available measurement trajectory, gravity, ray and memory arrive as a compound event. We can analytically unfold the event and construct separate models for its parts. This separation may be mathematically fruitful and technologically powerful. Yet success within the separated models does not retroactively show that the measured event was originally divided in the same way.

For me, this is where the ordinary demand for a yes-or-no answer becomes unhelpful. Is gravity light? The question already assumes that *gravity* and *light* name two completed objects whose identity can be compared. But the work here concerns the formation of those objects as symbols. Before we ask whether they are the same, we must ask how each became separately admissible and whether any measurement crossed the generonic boundary carrying only one of them.

At present, the stronger identity remains open. Perhaps future experiments will provide a means of differentiation that is not contained by the same observational dependence. Perhaps the language itself will have to change before such an experiment can be conceived. The admissible statement for now is more limited: every completed measurement of gravity contains a distinction-bearing interaction, registration and memory, and therefore does not demonstrate gravity’s independence from light.

This limitation is not a weakness. It tells us where the measurement ends and the symbolic extension begins. It allows the inquiry to remain open without converting openness into a licence to declare anything we wish.

## A finite symbolic reading

This is where my own work in Finite Symbolic Mechanics begins to enter the image, although I do not want to suggest that Blake was somehow describing a framework developed two centuries later. Rather, the framework gives me a language through which I can follow what the image appears to be doing.

The surrounding field contains differences and relations, but these have not yet been converted into the particular objects required by geometry. Light participates in making a distinction available. Newton’s body, eye and instruments select and stabilise certain relations. The dividers preserve a separation. The scroll receives a finite mark. Memory permits comparison. Only then can the registered trajectory be compressed into a symbolic entity.

The sequence is therefore not a world of already named objects followed by a faithful copy of those objects. It is closer to:

\[
\text{interactional field}
\longrightarrow
\text{distinction-bearing interaction}
\longrightarrow
\text{instrumental registration}
\longrightarrow
\text{retention and comparison}
\longrightarrow
\text{finite mark}
\longrightarrow
\text{symbolic entity}.
\]

At each passage something is retained and something is left outside. The finished symbol is powerful precisely because it does not attempt to carry everything. Gravity can enter a compact equation because the equation does not repeat the full history of observation each time the symbol appears. The problem begins only when this compression ceases to be recognised as compression.

The generonic boundary marks the transition at which an interaction becomes a finite registration within a symbolic stream. What crosses that boundary is not gravity as a perfectly formed noun. It is a detector change, displacement, timing relation, pixel, threshold crossing or other lowest-order registration, indexed to an instrument, protocol, uncertainty and provenance. The semantic entity *gravity* is constructed further downstream.

Blake’s achievement is to leave the removed trajectory in the picture. He gives us the body, hand, instrument, material line, curled scroll, rock, darkness and light. He allows Newton’s diagram to remain precise, but restores the diagram’s container. The viewer can see that gravity is held within an illuminated and remembered relation, even as Newton’s attention closes around the separated construction.

Perhaps Newton’s error, if we wish to call it an error, is not that he measures. It is that he does not turn after measuring. He does not look back towards the interaction through which the distinction became available, the material surface upon which the mark was made, or the light that permitted gravity to appear as an observable relation. Yet this should not be treated as a final judgement upon Newton the man. Blake’s figure is larger than biography. He is humanity as geometer, including ourselves whenever we forget the path by which our symbols were made.

## The ocean of language

There is another form in the image that I find increasingly difficult to unsee. The rock or reef can become the mouth of a great fish or oceanic creature. Newton seems to sit within its opening, perhaps even in the process of being swallowed. What appeared to be a passive support then becomes active. While Newton divides the world upon the scroll, the larger world is already enclosing him.

Perhaps this is the ocean of language. A mouth consumes, but it also speaks. Newton produces a language through which the world can be divided, measured and communicated, yet he is himself held within a much older and wider symbolic field. His nouns, lines and operations are not created from nowhere. They arrive through inherited speech, earlier diagrams, instruments, institutions, theological disputes and forms of life that the final equation does not contain.

The image may therefore hold one enclosure within another. The Euclidean diagram sits within the Newtonian frame; the Newtonian frame sits within the ocean of language; and the ocean itself sits within Blake’s image, which we now enter and reconstruct from our own position. No level gives us an untouched view from outside. Each act of reading becomes another trajectory through the field.

This is why I would hesitate to call the image anti-scientific. Blake does not make Newton weak or ridiculous. The geometry works within the surface prepared for it. The dividers can preserve an interval, and the line can support a repeatable calculation. The danger begins when this success is used to declare the surrounding measurement trajectory unnecessary, unreal or already explained.

## Newton’s names remain open

We have inherited from Newton a collection of extraordinarily successful names: force, mass, gravity, motion, space and light. They organise measurements, guide instruments and enable predictions. But their practical success does not mean that the questions gathered beneath them have been closed. In some respects, the success of the names may make their unresolved origins more difficult to see.

What is gravity? We can measure changes, write equations, predict trajectories and build technologies that rely upon those predictions. We can place one mathematical description beside another and judge their ranges of agreement. Yet every judgement returns us to registered differences, and every registration requires the coupled conditions through which a distinction becomes available and can be retained.

What is light? We describe it as corpuscle, wave, ray, field, photon, signal or measured interaction according to the symbolic and instrumental frame in use. Each description opens a productive path, but none arrives without a source, path, detector, comparison and finite mark. The word *light* is no more exempt from the problem than the word *gravity*.

It seems to me that we are still working inside the questions Newton helped to name. We have refined the measurements and greatly extended the calculations, but refinement is not the same as closure. The seventeenth-century debate did not end because one vocabulary became institutionally dominant. It changed form, and some of its uncertainties were placed beneath increasingly effective layers of symbolic construction.

Greene’s challenge therefore remains with us, even if we do not accept his theology or alternative natural philosophy. Pascal’s warning remains with us, even if we do not divide the mind exactly as he did. Blake’s Newton remains with us because the image does not supply a replacement theory. It asks us to turn and examine the conditions of our own seeing.

## The question returned by AI

The arrival of artificial intelligence and large language models makes this older debate newly visible. A language model measurably produces coherent text. It can continue an argument, translate between symbolic forms, write computer code and solve classes of mathematical problems. It can sometimes produce a trajectory that we judge insightful and at other times produce an equally coherent trajectory that has lost contact with its source. These are registered outputs from constructed systems, not merely hypothetical claims.

We are therefore pushed back towards questions that our inherited nouns appeared to have settled. What is meaning? What is understanding? What is a proof? Where does the direction of an argument reside? Is coherence evidence of an internal grasp, or is *grasp* itself a word formed from behaviours previously encountered only in human beings? If a system produces an admissible mathematical answer, what exactly are we measuring when we say that it has or has not understood the problem?

There is a temptation to answer too quickly in either direction. We may declare that the system understands because its text is coherent, or that it cannot understand because it is a machine. Both declarations risk returning inherited semantic entities to the world without reopening the trajectories that formed them. The word *understanding* carries human history, bodily action, social judgement, memory, intention and recognised consequence. A large language model enters some of these trajectories and not others. The boundary is not disclosed by assertion; it must be investigated through what the system measurably does, what it cannot preserve, and how its symbolic activity is coupled to ours.

Perhaps AI is our contemporary version of Blake’s scroll. We have built an immensely compressed symbolic surface from human language, and it now returns language to us in forms that appear coherent and directed. We look into that surface and ask whether there is meaning behind it, while often forgetting that *behind*, *meaning* and *system* are themselves inherited ways of partitioning the event.

The model and reader form a coupled symbolic trajectory. The prompt changes the output, the output changes the reader, and the next prompt emerges from that changed position. Meaning may not sit entirely inside either participant. It may arise within the relation while still depending upon bodies, machines, histories, measurements, electrical states, illuminated screens and institutions that the immediate text does not carry. Once again, the clean symbolic surface is supported by a much wider world.

This returns us to Blake. The words produced by a language model become observable through another chain of interaction, registration and display. We judge the symbols while often overlooking the material and historical field that has made them available. We may then ask whether the system understands as though *the system* and *understanding* were already independent objects waiting to be compared. Newton’s division is repeated upon a new scroll.

This does not give us a solution, and for me that is important. Finite Symbolic Mechanics is not offered as a final vocabulary that replaces Newton, Pascal, Greene or Blake. It is an attempt to keep the construction visible: to ask where the symbol began, what was registered, what was removed, what uncertainty remains, and by what agreement the resulting object became admissible. It may itself create new closures, and these must remain open to the same examination.

Blake’s Newton is therefore not safely behind us in the eighteenth century. He is present whenever a successful representation contracts the field of attention around itself. He is present in the equation, the scientific noun, the institutional consensus and now the language model. But the surrounding sea is also still present. The light has not been gathered completely into the line, the foot remains locked into the world, and the scroll still curls back into material extent.

Perhaps the task is not to abandon the diagram, but to remember to turn. We can retain the measurement while reopening its provenance. We can use Newton’s names while refusing to pretend that the names have ended the inquiry. We can engage with AI without deciding in advance that coherence either proves or disproves understanding.

The debate remains as alive now as it was in the seventeenth century because the underlying difficulty remains. We live within the world we attempt to describe, and every language through which we reach towards it is also one of the things that world has produced. We have not observed gravity outside the light through which its effects become distinguishable, just as we have not encountered meaning outside the interactions through which meaning becomes available to us.

There is no position outside the sea from which we can finally settle this. There is only the next interaction, the next registration, the next distinction and the next attempt to follow the trajectory without mistaking its compressed mark for the whole. Blake leaves Newton facing the line. He leaves the turning to us.
