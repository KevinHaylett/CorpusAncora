This is an excellent question, as Wittgenstein's view of mathematics is one of the most radical,
controversial, and misunderstood parts of his philosophy. He rejected almost all major theoretical
approaches to mathematics, both in his time and now.

Let's break it down. There are two main periods to his thought, with significant continuity on this
topic:

1. **Early Wittgenstein (** **_Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus_** **)**
2. **Late Wittgenstein (** **_Philosophical Investigations, Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics_** **)**

The _later_ view is his mature and most influential stance.

## Core Tenets of Wittgenstein's Later View of Mathematics

Think of this as a battle against what he saw as the "mythology" of mathematics.

**1. Mathematics is a "Motte" (a human activity), not a "Doctrine" (a body of truths about a
Platonic reality).**

```
Against Platonism: He fiercely denied that mathematical objects (numbers, sets, functions) exist
in a non-physical, timeless "third realm." Mathematicians do not discover pre-existing truths.
Against Psychologism: He also denied that mathematics is about the inner workings of the
human mind (e.g., our intuition of space or time).
His View: Mathematics is a grammatical activity. It consists of a patchwork of man-made
linguistic rules, conventions, and techniques. Doing math is like playing a game or following a
recipe. The "necessity" of a mathematical proof is the necessity of following a rule we have set up.
```
**2. The Meaning of a Mathematical Proposition is its Use.**

```
A number, or an equation like 2 + 2 = 4, doesn't gain its meaning by pointing to a mysterious
"fact" (2+2 pointing to the number  4 ).
Its meaning is the role it plays in our language-games: counting apples, distributing money,
measuring a room, building a bridge. The proposition is a norm (a rule for representation), not a
statement about a weird realm of abstract objects. It tells us how we will use words like "two,"
"plus," "two," and "four."
```

**3. The Status of Logic and Mathematical Truths.**

```
Not Synthetic A Priori (Kant): Mathematical truths are not discoveries about the structure of
experience.
Not Analytic (Logical Positivists): They are not true by virtue of meaning alone in a simple,
formal sense.
His View: "Grammatical" or "Rule-Descriptive." 2 + 2 = 4 is like a rule of grammar. It doesn't
describe the world, but it provides the scaffolding for how we describe the world. It
is hardened (fixed) because of its central role in our lives.
```
**4. The Role of Proof.**

```
Not a Discovery of a Pre-existing Connection: A proof does not reveal a hidden link between
concepts (like a railway line connecting two stations that were always there).
A New Move in the Game: A proof creates a new concept or a new rule. It changes the grammar
of our language. For example, proving the Pythagorean theorem gives us a new norm for judging
lengths of right-angled triangles. The conclusion of a proof is not a discovery; it is
the adoption of a new rule.
```
**5. The Surprising Role of Experiment & Pictures.**

```
He argued that an "experiment" in mathematics (e.g., trying to find a repeating pattern in π) is
not an experiment on numbers, but an experiment on the notation we use to represent numbers.
When we find that the pattern 777 appears in π, we haven't discovered a new property of a pre-
existing π, but a new feature of our decimal notation.
He famously used visual proofs (like a picture of two equal squares cut on the diagonal to form a
larger square) to argue that the proof is a picture that we see and then accept as a new rule.
```

# What Secretary Script Was

Secretary hand was the dominant handwriting style in England (and parts of Europe) throughout
the 1500s and well into the 1600s. It was the everyday script of:

```
Legal documents (court records, wills, property deeds)
Business correspondence (merchant letters, accounts)
Government administration (chanceries, royal courts)
Literary manuscripts (including plays and poetry)
```
If you were an educated person in Tudor England who needed to write something down — and you
weren't a nobleman using the fancier italic hand — you almost certainly wrote in secretary script.

# Why It Emerged

```
Read 9 web pages
```

The script developed out of a practical need. In the late 1400s, long-distance trade and government
administration were expanding rapidly. The older medieval book hands (like Anglicana) were too
slow and too formal for the volume of writing required. Secretary hand was:

```
Faster to write (cursive, flowing)
More legible than its predecessors (at least to trained readers)
Adaptable to English (rather than Latin, which dominated earlier scripts)
```
It was, in effect, a **productivity tool** for the information economy of the 16th century.

# The Visual Characteristics (And Why They're Tricky)

Secretary script is famous among paleographers (manuscript scholars) for being difficult to read
today. Some distinctive features:

Here's an example of the kind of form scholars look at — this is from a mid-Tudor secretary hand,
showing those characteristic 'z-form r' and 'cracked-egg s' shapes:

```
"This hand furnishes a textbook example of the consistent secretary treatment of double minuscule f
in this period... generally formed by a smallish, squat form of f joined to a larger, more stretched
and bolder form."
```
**Feature What It Looks Like**

**Long 's'** Looks like an 'f' without the crossbar — used at thebeginning or middle of words

**'sigma' s** A rounded terminal 's' that resembles the Greek sigma (ς)

**'cracked-egg' s** A specific form that died out mid-century — useful fordating manuscripts

**Z-shaped 'r'** A distinctive form that looks nothing like modern 'r'

**Stunted 'p'** A squat 'p' with a short descender
