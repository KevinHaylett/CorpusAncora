11.8. — We Don’t Really Understand Photons
In Section 11.6 (Episode 57), the poor analogy of raindrops was given to start
giving an idea of photons in relation to frequency and intensity of light and all
other frequencies of electromagnetic radiation but, of course, photons are not
objects with mass like raindrops. Typically, they are assumed to be some sort
of wave packet, which can be created by the superimposition upon one


another of different electromagnetic wavelengths. This is probably wrong, of
course, because those wavelengths are classical artefacts created by photons,
not the other way round.

‘A single photon is a particular relativistic massless wave-particle for which
scientists have still major difficulties to attribute a clear physical
representation. Historically, the scientific understanding of light’s properties,
guided each time by the interpretation of the experiments, was a continuous
balancing between the wave and particle natures.’ (Meis, 2023, p. 311)

‘By the late 1920’s the pivotal question was how to unify Maxwell’s wave
theory of light with its experimentally derived particle nature. The answer to
this question occupied Albert Einstein for the rest of his life and was solved in
quantum electrodynamics and its successor the Standard Model.’ (Wikipedia
on Photon, 2024)

Constantin Meis, in his 2023 paper, pointed to later studies, in the 1920’s,
where the photoelectric effect and Compton scattering was explained by
considering only the electromagnetic wave nature of light, without referring
to photons at all. Making the point photon even more questionable, Meis
cited experiments with microwaves in the 1950’s which showed no energy was
transmitted through apertures that were smaller than a quarter of a
wavelength and slightly later experiments, by Mandel in the 1960’s, which
concluded that a photon cannot be localised in a length shorter than its
wavelength and cannot be better localised than within a volume of the order
of a cube of its wavelength. Meis concluded, therefore, that:

‘the experimental evidence conflicts with both the point photon model, upon
which QED has been developed, and the classical continuous electromagnetic
wave theory, showing that light must be composed of localized “wave
packets” traveling in vacuum at the universal velocity c carrying energy hν and
momentum hν/c. In reality, the point photon concept has permitted to
establish an extremely efficient mathematical approach in QED for describing
states before and after an interaction processes. However, it is obvious that it
is inappropriate for the description of the real nature of a single photon state.’
(Meis, 2023, p. 313)

After more detailed mathematical analysis in the paper, Meis concluded that
‘the photon is not a point particle but rather a “wave-corpuscle”. Indeed, it is
readily demonstrated that the origin of Heisenberg’s uncertainty lays precisely
on the photon spatial properties.’ (Meis, 2023, p. 326)

If an electron changes from a high energy state to a lower one, there is a


transition from one orbital to another, as the electron orbital adjusts and
settles down to the new energy state. As both orbitals are defined by
wavefunctions, the photon that is emitted could logically be assumed to be
the subtraction of one wavefunction from another, the change of energy
emitted in the form of “wave-corpuscle” of electromagnetic radiation.

We know an electromagnetic wave, that gives a line on the emission
spectrum, like shown in Figure 21, is made up of millions of photons, with
each photon having an “action” (energy x time) which is the Planck constant.
The electron in an atom is not a minuscule cannonball, and the photon is not
a particle either and has a cloud of probability around some of its properties.
We think of a photon interacting with an electron, when neither are particles
in a classical physics sense. So, we have to stop trying to interpret the photon
in classical terms, as it really is a relativistic epiphenomenon conveying a unit
of quantum electromagnetic action in the universe. It is neither a particle nor
a wave. The combination of all those millions of millions of photons travelling
through space give us the classical electromagnetic wave under the
correspondence principle.

It is also worth being reminded that the scale of a hydrogen atom is a radius
of 0.25 angstrom, where an angstrom is a tenth of a nanometre and larger
atoms are around one angstrom or a little more, whereas the wavelength of
visible light is between 4000 and 7000 angstrom. The photon interacts with an
electron and those are five orders of magnitude smaller than the angstrom
unit, called a fermi or femtometre (see Figure 19 of Episode 56). How this
comparatively large thing that Meis refers to, moving at the speed of light,
acts upon a submicroscopic electron is, perhaps, part of the mystery of the
relationship between quantum mechanics and relativity or it may just be a
particular case of collapse of the photon’s wavefunction. We may need
another Einstein to explain it.

In our perspective, photons travel in a straight line in the geometry of flat
space-time and curve in response to gravity (they have momentum which has
inertia) and we interpret them by the varying degree of the cyclic electric and
magnetic force energy emitted as they move past in their millions, which
aggregated together reveals the wave form that we call the electromagnetic
wave. Thus, an individual photon exhibits both the characteristic of a particle
and a wave and the frequency of the electromagnetic wave relates to how
much energy is embodied in those photons.

There is an ongoing debate about what is more fundamental, fields or


particles. The Standard Model of particle physics (which still uses the term
particle, although these days it is quantum field theory) is based upon fields
being really fundamental and bosons (which includes the photon of the
electromagnetic field) are virtual particles, but this rubs up against the current
science textbooks and popular science which tend to have the photon particle
as fundamentally the way we understand light. Thus, while we are normally
told that light is both wavelike and particle-like, the tendency is to imply that
the particle interpretation is more fundamental. While the electromagnetic
field doesn’t require a medium for its transmission, quantum physicists think
of it like an ocean of small harmonic oscillators (like a mass on a spring),
corresponding to each frequency of the radiation. What we call a photon is a
wave of energy travelling across the ocean (field) which can only carry energy
at certain quantum levels. Those tiny increments in quantum levels
correspond to the sources of transmission, like an electron dropping from one
orbital to a lower one in an atom. Thus, when we see the colour red, it means
that the appropriate photon receptors in our eyes are experiencing the
discharge of energy from a sufficient number of photons of the red
wavelength (determined by the frequency and the speed of light).

‘Photons obey the laws of quantum mechanics, and so their behaviour has
both wave-like and particle-like aspects. When a photon is detected by a
measuring instrument, it is registered as a single, particulate unit. However,
the probability of detecting a photon is calculated by equations that describe
waves. This combination of aspects is known as wave–particle duality. For
example, the probability distribution for the location at which a photon might
be detected displays clearly wave-like phenomena such as diffraction and
interference. A single photon passing through a double slit has its energy
received at a point on the screen with a probability distribution given by its

experiments confirm that the photon is not a short pulse of electromagnetic

spread out as it propagates, nor does this energy divide when it encounters a
beam splitter. Rather, the received photon acts like a point-like particle since it
is absorbed or emitted as a whole by arbitrarily small systems, including
systems much smaller than its wavelength, such as an atomic nucleus (≈10–
m across) or even the point-like electron.

While many introductory texts treat photons using the mathematical
techniques of non-relativistic quantum mechanics, this is in some ways an
awkward oversimplification, as photons are by nature intrinsically relativistic.
Because photons have zero rest mass, no wave function defined for a photon
can have all the properties familiar from wave functions in non-relativistic
quantum mechanics. In order to avoid these difficulties, physicists employ the


second-quantized theory of photons ../.., quantum electrodynamics, in which
photons are quantized excitations of electromagnetic modes.’ (Wikipedia on
Photon, 2024)

We discussed the principle of least action in Chapter 9 (Episodes 41–45) and,
as was also mentioned then, we shall later discuss how Planck’s constant
seems to be the universe’s “least action”. Let’s go back to the quotation that
Chapter 9 started with, to deal with this mathematically defined term “action”
in physics, rather than the general looser sense of the word.

‘To put it simply, it’s a bit like power, which is what we men are interested in
when looking at a car or motorbike engine. Power is the energy spent or
delivered per second, so its dimension is J/s [Joules/second], not J.s
[Joules.seconds for action]. However, your mind can see the similarity in
thinking here. Energy is a nice concept, be it potential (think of a water bucket

Power typically relates to a continuum of operational output rather than a
single action, whereas action is the product of the energy involved and the
time duration of the action, so the punch is more an action in physics than
having a measure of power, as such. Nature seeks to minimise action within
its own realm, as discussed in Chapter 9.

We know from Planck’s experiments that combining that constant (h) with
frequency of electromagnetic radiation gives the energy of an individual
photon (E = hf) and it keeps that energy unless it “impacts” with something.
We also know that the energy of an electromagnetic wave is proportional to
the sum of the squares of the electric and magnetic wave’s field amplitudes.
Frequency just defines the wavelength and the time period of a wave cycle
that repeats as it moves along at the speed of light. Consequently, if we were
to plot one cycle of the waveform as the amplitude squared against time, then
the area under that curve must be proportional to the originating action (both
halves of the squared sine wave are positive) and this action’s value is always
some integer multiple of the Planck’s constant for a photon (h = E/f = Et
where t is the time of one wave cycle). Indeed, all photons seem to involve the
same action in space-time, as what we refer to as energy gets higher with
frequency and this is just due to the fact that the same action is packed into a
shorter timeframe.


The overall intensity of a beam of one of the colours of visible light (or at any
other particular frequency of electromagnetic radiation) is the number of
photons in the beam at that particular frequency travelling together. A beam
could be of mixed frequencies like that of “white light” and so then the
intensities are in combination. Photons only exists as moving virtual particles.

with matter by being absorbed or emitted. In the lower frequencies, up to
visible light, typically the interaction happens by causing or resulting from
