\section{Introduction: Computation as Trajectory}

A computation is often described as an operation on numbers. This
description is useful, but it is also heavily compressed. It hides the symbolic
preparation that makes the operation possible, the representational
constraints that define what a number is allowed to be, the physical substrate
that carries the symbolic distinction, and the measurement process by which
the result is made available. In ordinary language, we say that a computer
``adds two numbers,'' or that a quantum computer ``uses qubits,'' or that an
optical computer ``computes with light.'' Each phrase is functional, but each
phrase also conceals a longer symbolic trajectory.

Finite Symbolic Mechanics begins by slowing this language down. A number
is not merely a number. A bit is not merely a bit. A qubit is not simply a
quantum object. A CCD image is not merely a picture. Each is a finite symbolic
structure held within a larger symbolic, physical, historical, and measurement-
facing trajectory. A computation, in this framing, is not only a change of state.
It is a controlled unfolding of symbolic relations.

We may therefore begin with the following broad form:
\begin{equation}
M_0 \rightarrow T_1(M_0) \rightarrow T_2(T_1(M_0)) \rightarrow
M_{\mathrm{out}},
\end{equation}
where $M_0$ is an initial symbolic configuration or map, $T_1$ and $T_2$ are
transformations, and $M_{\mathrm{out}}$ is the resulting output
configuration. This expression is deliberately broad. It may describe a binary
register being transformed by logic gates, a quantum state being transformed
by unitary operators, or a finite symbolic map being transformed into another
finite symbolic map.

The chapter compares three computational grammars. The first is
conventional binary computation. Here symbols are carried by binary
distinctions, usually represented as $0$ and $1$, and transformed through
explicit logic, arithmetic, memory addressing, shifting, branching, and storage.
The second is quantum ket computation. In the standard circuit model,
quantum computation is described using quantum states, gates, circuits, and
measurements \cite{NielsenChuang2010,Deutsch1985,Feynman1982}. In this
chapter, the ket is not treated as a direct image of reality, but as a
compressed symbolic directory for a coupled experimental-computational
trajectory. The third is FSM / Alphonic map computation. This is the new
proposal explored here. In this approach, computation is not primarily
performed by sequential binary operations or by Hilbert-space ket evolution,
but by preloading stable symbolic relations into finite maps. A transformation
then activates these relations, and a detection layer reads an output map. The
system may behave partly like a lookup machine, but not merely as a
conventional table. It is a relational map engine in which many functional


symbolic trajectories may be unfolded in parallel.

The central hinge of this chapter is simple:
\begin{equation}
\text{Computation is not only symbol manipulation.}
\end{equation}
It is also:
\begin{equation}
\text{symbol preparation}
\rightarrow
\text{trajectory constraint}
\rightarrow
\text{relational transformation}
\rightarrow
\text{measurement/readout}
\rightarrow
\text{decoded symbolic result}.
\end{equation}

This shift matters because it allows binary computers, quantum computers,
optical systems, CCD arrays, lookup engines, and Alphonic map engines to be
compared without forcing them into the same symbolic basin too early. Each
may compute, but each computes by holding and unfolding symbolic
function differently.

\section{Finite Symbols and Functional Symbolic Trajectories}

A finite symbol is a bounded, distinguishable symbolic unit available inside
the endogenous symbolic space. Examples include:
\begin{equation}
0,\quad 1,\quad |0\rangle,\quad |1\rangle,\quad a_{ij},\quad b_{ij},\quad
c_{ij},\quad A,\quad B,\quad C.
\end{equation}

A finite symbol is not assumed to correspond directly to an exogenous thing.
It may have been produced through measurement, through convention,
through symbolic construction, through apparatus calibration, or through
computational encoding. Once it exists in the endogenous symbolic space, it
is held by other symbols. It becomes part of a wider symbolic flow.

A functional symbolic trajectory is a finite symbolic pathway that carries a
useful relation, prediction, operation, transformation, or measurement-facing
function. A binary addition circuit is a functional symbolic trajectory. A
quantum circuit is a functional symbolic trajectory. An image transformation
from $A$ and $B$ to $C$ is a functional symbolic trajectory. A paragraph
defining a concept is also a functional symbolic trajectory.

The important point is that many different symbolic trajectories may carry the
same function. The same arithmetic result may be obtained by binary
addition, lookup table, analogue circuit, optical transform, neural network
approximation, or quantum subroutine. The function is not uniquely bound to


one symbolic pathway.

This prevents premature correspondence. If a quantum formalism works, that
does not mean the formalism is uniquely identical to the exogenous system. If
a binary algorithm works, that does not mean binary logic is the natural form
of the relation being computed. If an image-based map engine works, that
does not mean the map is ``the thing itself.'' In each case, the system has
stabilised a functional symbolic trajectory.

FSM therefore does not ask first, ``what thing is this?'' It asks: what symbolic
trajectory is being used? What function does it carry? Where was the symbol
produced? What has been compressed? What has been flattened? What is
being measured? What uncertainty remains?

This language is especially useful when approaching quantum computation,
because quantum language often moves quickly from symbols to things. A
ket becomes ``the state.'' A wavefunction becomes ``the particle.'' A
measurement result becomes ``what happened.'' FSM slows this down. The
ket may be useful, extraordinarily useful, but within FSM it remains a finite
symbolic structure embedded in a larger functional trajectory.

\section{The Generonic Boundary as Coupling Layer}

In earlier FSM language, the Generonic Boundary can be described as the
process by which exogenous interaction becomes finite symbol:
\begin{equation}
\text{Exogenous interaction}
\rightarrow
\text{Generonic boundary}
\rightarrow
\text{finite symbol}.
\end{equation}

This is the measurement-facing direction. Something outside the endogenous
symbolic space perturbs the measurement system, and a finite symbol
appears inside the symbolic system. A detector clicks. A pixel changes value. A
voltage crosses a threshold. A bit is written. A result enters the laboratory
notebook.

For computation, this one-way description is incomplete. Symbols do not
merely receive measurements. Symbols also guide actions. They define
experiments. They determine what apparatus is built, what source is activated,
what detector is placed, what voltage is applied, what gate is implemented,
what pulse is shaped, what threshold is selected, and what result counts as
valid.

So the Generonic Boundary is better described here as a coupling layer:
\begin{equation}
\text{Endogenous symbols}
\leftrightarrow
\text{Generonic coupling layer}


\leftrightarrow
\text{Exogenous interaction}.
\end{equation}

This is not a claim that symbols magically alter the exogenous world. It is the
simpler and more practical claim that symbols guide physical preparation, and
physical preparation constrains later symbolic measurement. In computation,
this coupling becomes explicit. A program drives hardware. Hardware returns
symbols. The returned symbols alter the next program state.

This also clarifies the role of measurement. A measurement is not merely
``data added to theory.'' A measurement enters the endogenous space as an
unstable finite symbol. It may sit at a saddle region of the existing symbolic
flow. It may be absorbed by the current model, rejected as error, averaged
away as noise, renamed, stabilised, or used to open a new trajectory.

A useful form is:
\begin{equation}
\text{Measurement} \rightarrow R_1,
\end{equation}
where $R_1$ is the first measured result. But $R_1$ rarely stands alone.
Typically, an initial result leads to, or points toward, a following result:
\begin{equation}
R_1 \rightarrow R_2.
\end{equation}

However, once $R_1$ enters the endogenous symbolic space, it is
immediately held by all other symbols in that space:
\begin{equation}
R_1 \mid \Ssym,
\end{equation}
where $\Ssym$ is the surrounding symbolic system: prior theory, apparatus
history, calibration practice, language, mathematical expectation, uncertainty,
and possible future trajectories.

This gives an important FSM statement: a measurement enters FSM as an
unstable finite symbol at a saddle region of the existing symbolic trajectory.
Typically, an initial result leads to, or points toward, a following result. Once
inside the endogenous space, however, the symbol is held by prior symbols,
possible future symbols, apparatus history, interpretive language, and the
wider symbolic flow.

This matters for computation because every computational result is also a
measured symbol. A binary output, a quantum measurement, a CCD pixel
array, or an Alphonic output map all become meaningful only after crossing
the coupling layer into the endogenous symbolic system.

\section{Binary Computation as Explicit Symbolic Transformation}

Conventional binary computation is the most familiar computational
grammar. It stores and manipulates finite symbolic distinctions represented


as:
\begin{equation}
0,\quad 1.
\end{equation}

A binary register may be written as:
\begin{equation}
B = (b_1,b_2,b_3,\ldots,b_n),
\end{equation}
where each $b_i$ is a binary symbol and $n$ is the number of binary positions
in the register.

A logic operation transforms one symbolic configuration into another:
\begin{equation}
B_0 \xrightarrow{L} B_1,
\end{equation}
where $L$ is a logical, arithmetic, memory, or control operation. A sequence
of operations becomes:
\begin{equation}
B_0 \xrightarrow{L_1} B_1 \xrightarrow{L_2} B_2 \xrightarrow{L_3} \cdots
\xrightarrow{L_k} B_k.
\end{equation}

Binary computation has enormous strengths. It is general, robust,
programmable, scalable, and deeply engineered. It supports memory,
branching, iteration, modularity, abstraction, error detection, error correction,
and symbolic layering. Modern computing civilisation is built from this
grammar.

Yet binary computation also carries a cost. Many relations must be computed
explicitly each time they are required. Even when a relation is stable,
repeatable, and known in advance, a binary system may still need to derive it
through sequential or parallel logic operations unless the relation has been
stored.

A lookup table shifts this balance. Instead of recomputing:
\begin{equation}
f(a,b),
\end{equation}
the system stores the result and retrieves it:
\begin{equation}
(a,b)\rightarrow f(a,b).
\end{equation}

This already hints at the FSM / Alphonic direction. A lookup table is not less
computational because it retrieves rather than derives. It has simply moved
the cost. The cost was paid during table construction, storage, addressing, and
validation. The live computational act becomes selection and retrieval.

This gives a first useful distinction: binary computation often computes
relations each time they are needed; lookup-based computation stores prior


relational structure and activates it when required. The Alphonic map idea
extends this logic, but replaces the single lookup table with a stable symbolic
map structure capable of holding many relations in parallel.

\section{Quantum Ket Computation in Standard Language}

Quantum computation is usually formulated in the language of states, gates,
circuits, and measurements. In the circuit model, a system is prepared,
transformed by quantum gates, and measured
\cite{NielsenChuang2010,Deutsch1985,Feynman1982}.

For a single qubit, a pure state is written:
\begin{equation}
|\psi\rangle = \alpha |0\rangle + \beta |1\rangle,
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are complex amplitudes and:
\begin{equation}
|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1.
\end{equation}

For $n$ qubits, the state is written:
\begin{equation}
|\psi\rangle =
\sum_{i=0}^{2^n-1} c_i |i\rangle,
\end{equation}
where $c_i \in \mathbb{C}$, $|i\rangle$ are computational basis states, and:
\begin{equation}
\sum_i |c_i|^2 = 1.
\end{equation}

A quantum circuit applies transformations to this state. In the simplest pure-
state form:
\begin{equation}
|\psi_0\rangle
\rightarrow
U_1|\psi_0\rangle
\rightarrow
U_2U_1|\psi_0\rangle
\rightarrow
\cdots
\rightarrow
|\psi_f\rangle
\rightarrow
\text{measurement}.
\end{equation}

Here $U_1, U_2, \ldots$ are unitary operators representing quantum gates. In
practice, quantum computing also involves density matrices, channels, noise,
decoherence, hardware constraints, compilation, calibration, and
measurement. State preparation itself is a substantial issue: the preparation of
useful quantum states is an active topic because quantum algorithms depend


on how states are initialised, encoded, and measured
\cite{PleschBrukner2011,Mottonen2005}.

The standard language is operationally powerful. It predicts measurable
distributions. It provides a formal grammar for designing algorithms. It
enables simulation, compilation, circuit optimisation, and hardware control.

But FSM asks us not to skip the symbolic layers. The ket is not the
experimental system itself. It is a compressed symbolic representation used to
coordinate preparation, transformation, prediction, and measurement. The
qubit is not merely a small ``thing.'' It is a flattened symbolic geometry. It
stands in for a long experimental-symbolic trajectory.

A physical qubit may involve superconducting circuits, trapped ions, photons,
spins, defects, or other systems. But in the computational formalism, this
richness is compressed into $|0\rangle$, $|1\rangle$, amplitudes, gates, and
measurements. The qubit appears simple because much of the geometry has
already been folded away.

\text{unitary evolution}
\rightarrow
\text{measurement}
\rightarrow
\text{classical output}.
\end{equation}

In FSM / NLD language:

\rightarrow
\text{symbolic flattening}
\rightarrow

The correspondences are as follows. State preparation becomes

coupling}. Measurement becomes \emph{symbolic flattening}. The ket

This reframing matters because it avoids the misleading popular phrase that a
quantum computer ``tries all answers at once.'' The more careful statement is
that a prepared coupled state evolves as a whole under transformation rules,

The quantum computer is therefore not simply a parallel binary machine. It is
not performing many ordinary classical calculations side by side. It is an

In FSM notation:
\begin{equation}
\text{Quantum advantage}
\sim
\text{useful transformation of a preloaded coupled trajectory before

\end{equation}

The word \emph{preloaded} is important. A quantum computer does not
begin from nothing. It begins from a prepared state, an encoding, an ansatz,
an input distribution, a circuit design, and a measurement scheme. The later
evolution is constrained by this preparation. In FSM language, the initial ket-
state establishes the symbolic-physical conditions under which the later
coupled evolution can unfold.

This brings quantum computing closer to other forms of computation. It has a
different grammar, but not a magical exemption from preparation,
representation, coupling, measurement, and error correction.

\section{The Qubit as Flattened Geometry}

+
e^{i\phi}\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)|1\rangle,

where $\theta$ and $\phi$ define a point on the Bloch sphere. This partially

\text{apparatus coupling}

\QM \sim f(\FSM),

Within FSM, this is inescapable. Once quantum mechanics is expressed using

This is not a demotion of quantum mechanics. It is a clarification of its

\section{Stable Images and Parallel Symbolic Arrays}

We now shift from ket computation to map computation.

Suppose we have a stable image $A$, a stable image $B$, and a resulting
stable image $C$, generated by an experimental setup:
\begin{equation}
(A,B)\rightarrow C.
\end{equation}


The images may be arbitrary. They may be light patterns, sensor maps,
projected symbols, numeric encodings, spatial masks, CCD frames, or other
finite symbolic arrays. What matters is not that they are pictures in the
ordinary sense. What matters is that they are stable symbolic maps.

Let:
\begin{equation}
A=\{a_{ij}\},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
B=\{b_{ij}\},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
C=\{c_{ij}\},
\end{equation}
where $i$ and $j$ are finite spatial indices.

In an ordinary image-processing frame, $C$ might be treated as an output
picture. In FSM, $C$ is an output symbolic relation field. Each output element
may be treated as:
\begin{equation}
c_{ij}=f_{ij}(A,B),
\end{equation}
where $f_{ij}$ is the local or nonlocal function that maps the input maps $A$
and $B$ into the output element $c_{ij}$.

The family of output functions is:
\begin{equation}
\Fmap_{AB\rightarrow C}=\{f_{ij}\}.
\end{equation}

This is the key shift. The output map is not merely a grid of values. It is an
array of functional symbolic relations.

A CCD or CMOS detector is already a massively parallel measurement surface.
Each pixel can integrate light during an exposure and return a finite symbol.
Optical computing, photonic computing, and optical information processing
have long explored the use of light and optical systems for computation and
information transformation
\cite{Goodman2005,PsaltisMok1995,WoodsNaughton2012}. Diffractive optical
neural networks are another related field, using diffractive layers and optical
propagation to perform learned transformations \cite{Lin2018}.

However, the FSM / Alphonic proposal is not simply ``optical computing.'' It
shifts the emphasis.

Existing optical computing often says:
\begin{equation}
\text{light performs computation}.
\end{equation}


The FSM map proposal says:
\begin{equation}
\text{light creates or activates maps},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{the maps hold the computational compression},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{the output is a parallel symbolic relation field}.
\end{equation}

This distinction matters. The optics may be the coupling layer. The CCD may
be the readout layer. But the computational leverage lies in the maps and
their preloaded relational structure.

\section{The Alphonic Map Engine}

The Alphonic map engine is a proposed computational grammar that does
not begin from binary shift registers. It begins from finite symbolic maps and
stable map transformations.

The basic form is:
\begin{equation}
(A,B)\xrightarrow{T}C,
\end{equation}
where $A$ and $B$ are input maps, $T$ is a stable transformation or coupling
operation, and $C$ is an output map.

The output map may contain many preconfigured relations at once. If $A$
holds a number $a$, and $B$ holds a number $b$, then different regions or
layers of $C$ could hold:
\begin{equation}
a+b,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
a-b,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
ab,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\frac{a}{b},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
a>b,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
a=b,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
a^2+b^2,


\end{equation}
or other functions chosen by the map design.

This resembles lookup, but it is not merely a conventional lookup table. It is a
light-triggered relational lookup array, or more broadly, a trajectory-based
map computation.

The ``program'' is partly in the predefined maps and partly in the stable
transformation layer. Once the maps are defined and the system is calibrated,
the activation event unfolds many relational outputs in parallel.

A useful expression is:
\begin{equation}
(A,B,\Fmap)\xrightarrow{T}C,
\end{equation}
where $\Fmap$ is the preloaded family of relations available to the
transformation. The computation is not performed by deriving every relation
step by step at runtime. The relational structure has already been placed into
the maps. Runtime becomes activation, detection, and decoding.

This gives the central practical claim: the computational function is held in the
predefined map relations. The physical system supplies fast activation and
detection. The practical question is whether the cost of defining, storing,
stabilising, and decoding the maps is lower than computing the same relation
through binary operations.

This is a trade-off, not a miracle. The Alphonic engine does not automatically
replace binary computation. It may be advantageous where the cost of map
preparation and detection is lower than repeated binary derivation for a
useful class of tasks.

\section{The Three Computational Approaches}

We can now compare the three computational grammars.

In binary computation, the symbolic unit is the bit:
\begin{equation}
0,\quad 1.
\end{equation}
The storage medium is the register, memory cell, address space, or binary
array. The transformations are logic gates, arithmetic units, shifts, masks,
comparisons, branches, and memory operations. The strength of the system is
generality, reliability, programmability, and mature engineering. The weakness
is that relations often have to be computed explicitly, even when they are
stable and repeatedly used.

In FSM language, binary computation is:
\begin{equation}
\text{sequential or parallel binary symbolic trajectory}.
\end{equation}


In quantum ket computation, the symbolic unit is the ket:
\begin{equation}
|\psi\rangle,\quad |0\rangle,\quad |1\rangle,\quad \sum_i c_i|i\rangle.
\end{equation}
The storage medium is the prepared quantum state, held through a physical
system and represented through Hilbert-space formalism. The
transformations are unitary gates, quantum channels, controlled operations,
measurement procedures, and error-correction structures. The strength is the
ability to transform a coupled state representation before measurement. The
weakness is fragility, noise, preparation cost, readout cost, and heavy error-
correction overhead \cite{Preskill2018}.

In FSM language, quantum computation is:
\begin{equation}
\text{preloaded coupled ket trajectory}.
\end{equation}

In FSM / Alphonic map computation, the symbolic unit is the finite symbolic
map:
\begin{equation}
A,\quad B,\quad C,\quad \Fmap_{AB\rightarrow C}.
\end{equation}
The storage medium is the map relation itself. The transformation is:
\begin{equation}
(A,B)\xrightarrow{T}C.
\end{equation}
The strength is that many preconfigured relations may be activated and
detected in parallel. The weakness is map definition cost, calibration, error
correction, resolution limits, detector noise, source drift, decoding complexity,
and possibly limited generality unless the maps are richly reconfigurable.

In FSM language, Alphonic map computation is:
\begin{equation}
\text{trajectory-based relational map computation}.
\end{equation}

Compressed into one comparison:
\begin{equation}
\text{Binary computation: compute the relation.}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{Quantum computation: evolve the coupled trajectory.}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{FSM map computation: activate the preloaded relation field.}
\end{equation}

This is not a hierarchy. It is a comparison of symbolic grammars.

\section{Relation to Reservoir Computing and Optical Computing}


The FSM map proposal sits near several existing fields, but it does not
collapse neatly into them.

Reservoir computing is especially relevant. Reservoir computing uses a
dynamical system, called the reservoir, whose internal state is driven by input;
a readout layer is then trained to extract the desired output
\cite{Jaeger2001,LukoseviciusJaeger2009}. Photonic reservoir computing has
also been developed as a hardware route, with photonic platforms used as
physical reservoirs \cite{Larger2017,Tanaka2019}.

This is close to the FSM direction, because both approaches exploit a physical
or symbolic dynamical system rather than imposing all computation through
explicit binary logic. Reservoir computing says: let the system's dynamics
expand the input into a rich state, then train a readout.

FSM map computation says something adjacent but not identical: define
stable symbolic maps so that the relation field itself carries preloaded
computational structure. The physical transformation activates a map relation
rather than acting as the whole computational explanation.

Diffractive optical neural networks are also close. They use optical
propagation through diffractive structures to implement learned
transformations \cite{Lin2018}. Again, FSM differs in emphasis. It does not
deny that optics may transform the signal. It instead asks where the symbolic
compression lives. In many optical systems, the optical path, mask, lens,
diffractive layer, source, and detector jointly create the transformation. FSM
asks whether the maps themselves can be treated as the main computational
substrate.

So the FSM formulation is:
\begin{equation}
\text{optics} = \text{map-producing coupling layer},
\end{equation}
not necessarily:
\begin{equation}
\text{optics} = \text{the whole computer}.
\end{equation}

This is a subtle but important distinction. The optics create, select, combine, or
activate maps. The computation is read in the stable relation between maps.

\section{Stable Source Systems and Near-Field Map Formation}

The near-field light-source experiment offers a useful conceptual bridge.

Suppose a single LED or light source produces a stable pattern over time. The
first few detections are not merely isolated data points. They are early samples
of a stable unfolding trajectory:
\begin{equation}
D_1 \rightarrow D_2 \rightarrow D_3 \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow P,
\end{equation}


where $D_1, D_2, D_3$ are early detections and $P$ is the later stabilised
pattern.

If the system is stable, the early detections already point toward the later full
pattern. They do not predict it magically. Rather, they are finite samples of an
unfolding stable trajectory.

Now consider two nearby sources:
\begin{equation}
S_1 \rightarrow P_1,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
S_2 \rightarrow P_2.
\end{equation}
If both sources are activated together:
\begin{equation}
S_1 + S_2 \rightarrow P_{12}.
\end{equation}

The central comparison becomes:
\begin{equation}
\Delta P = P_{12} - (P_1 + P_2).
\end{equation}

If:
\begin{equation}
\Delta P \approx 0,
\end{equation}
then the system is mostly additive. If $\Delta P$ has stable structure, the
combined activation has produced a non-additive map relation.

This is not merely an optical question. It is a symbolic-computational
question. If stable source systems can produce stable non-additive maps, then
those maps may be usable as computational relation fields. A coupled light-
source system, paired with CCD detection, becomes a testbed for map-based
computation.

The critical issue is stability. If the array is stable, then the parallel symbolic
relations are stable. Each pixel-symbol is not merely an independent number.
It is held by neighbouring pixels, optical spread, diffraction, sensor geometry,
exposure time, source behaviour, thresholding, and the global pattern. The
array becomes:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{A}=\{p_i,\; r_{ij},\; R_{\mathrm{global}}\},
\end{equation}
where $p_i$ are pixel-symbols, $r_{ij}$ are inter-element relations, and
$R_{\mathrm{global}}$ is the stabilised whole-array relation.

The calculation is therefore not only in the pixel values. It is in the stable
relational geometry across the array.


\section{Preloading, Lookup, and the Cost of Computation}

Preloading is not unique to FSM map computation. Quantum computers
preload through state preparation. Binary computers preload through
programs, memory, constants, lookup tables, caches, models, compiled
instructions, and hardware design. Optical systems preload through masks,
lenses, diffractive layers, spatial light modulators, alignment, and calibration.

The question is not whether preloading occurs. The question is where the cost
is paid and where the compression lives.

For binary computation, the cost may be expressed roughly as:
\begin{equation}
\Cbin = C_{\mathrm{operation}} \times N_{\mathrm{steps}},
\end{equation}
where $C_{\mathrm{operation}}$ is the cost of each operation and
$N_{\mathrm{steps}}$ is the number of operations required.

For FSM map computation:
\begin{equation}
\Cfsm =
C_{\mathrm{map\ setup}}
+
C_{\mathrm{activation}}
+
C_{\mathrm{detection}}
+
C_{\mathrm{decoding}}
+
C_{\mathrm{correction}}.
\end{equation}

The map engine is advantageous when:
\begin{equation}
\Cfsm < \Cbin
\end{equation}
for a useful class of repeated or parallel tasks.

This is the correct trade-off. The FSM / Alphonic engine does not evade
computation. It relocates it. It pays cost in map design, map storage,
calibration, stability, and decoding, then seeks advantage through rapid
activation and parallel detection.

This may be especially relevant for functions that are stable, repeated,
spatially representable, relation-heavy, parallelisable, lookup-friendly, tolerant
of finite uncertainty, or naturally expressed as maps. It may be less useful for
tasks requiring arbitrary symbolic branching, complex dynamic control, exact
sequential dependency, or frequent reconfiguration.

The honest claim is therefore: FSM map computation may be powerful where
preloaded relational structure can be activated and detected faster than the


same relation can be derived through binary operations.

\section{Error Correction and Uncertainty}

Any serious computational proposal must address error.

Quantum computing requires error correction because physical quantum
systems are noisy and difficult to maintain \cite{Preskill2018,Shor1995}. FSM
map computation will also require correction. Possible error sources include
map instability, source drift, detector noise, pixel defects, alignment changes,
nonlinear saturation, temperature effects, exposure variation, thresholding
error, cross-talk between map regions, decoding ambiguity, and finite
resolution.

This is not a fatal objection. It is a design requirement.

An output map may be written not merely as:
\begin{equation}
C=\{c_{ij}\},
\end{equation}
but as:
\begin{equation}
C=\{(c_{ij},u_{ij})\},
\end{equation}
where $u_{ij}$ is local uncertainty associated with the output symbol $c_{ij}$.

A stable transformation may be defined by repeated outputs remaining within
an accepted bound:
\begin{equation}
d(C_k,C_l)<\epsilon,
\end{equation}
where $d$ is a chosen distance measure between output maps, $C_k$ and
$C_l$ are outputs from repeated runs, and $\epsilon$ is the tolerated
uncertainty.

Correction methods could include repeated exposures, calibration maps,
redundancy, neighbourhood voting, attractor classification, confidence maps,
difference maps, and stable basin decoding.

A difference map is especially important:
\begin{equation}
\Delta C = C_{\mathrm{measured}} - C_{\mathrm{expected}}.
\end{equation}

This allows the system to detect drift, noise, or unexpected coupling structure.
In FSM language, error correction is not merely technical cleanup. It is the
process by which unstable measured symbols are stabilised within the
endogenous symbolic flow.

\section{Simulation Before Hardware}


Before building physical hardware, FSM map computation can be simulated.

Quantum computers are often simulated by representing state vectors or
density matrices and applying gate operations. FSM map computation can be
simulated in an analogous broad sense, though not using Hilbert-space kets.
The simulator would represent finite symbolic maps and apply map
transformations:
\begin{equation}
M_0
\rightarrow
T_1(M_0)
\rightarrow
T_2(T_1(M_0))
\rightarrow
M_{\mathrm{out}}.
\end{equation}

Or, for the two-map case:
\begin{equation}
(A,B)\xrightarrow{T}C.
\end{equation}

The simulator would test whether maps can encode useful relation families,
how many functions can be held per output map, how noise affects decoding,
how lookup speed compares with binary calculation, how compression scales,
and what classes of tasks suit map computation.

This is an important practical step because the concept does not require
immediate physical implementation. The symbolic map grammar can be
developed first. Hardware then becomes a possible instantiation of a
previously tested symbolic-computational model.

This also matches the wider FSM principle: do not assume correspondence. A
software FSM map simulator is not the physical Alphonic engine. It is a
symbolic trajectory used to explore whether the formalism is coherent, stable,
and useful.

\section{Relation to Takens, NLD, and Phase-Space Reconstruction}

The nonlinear-dynamical framing is not decorative. It is foundational.

Takens-style embedding ideas are relevant because they show how dynamical
structure can be reconstructed from observed signals under suitable
conditions \cite{Takens1981,KantzSchreiber2004}. FSM does not simply import
Takens' theorem as a finished answer. Rather, it draws a methodological
lesson: observed symbolic sequences may carry more of the underlying
dynamical structure than they appear to carry when treated as isolated points.

This applies to language, measurement, image formation, quantum readout,
and map computation.


A measured symbol is not just a point. It may be a trajectory sample. An
image is not just an array. It may be a relational field. A qubit is not just a two-
state object. It may be a compressed symbolic directory for a coupled
trajectory. A computation is not just an operation. It may be the unfolding of a
preloaded trajectory through a constrained symbolic space.

The Alphonic engine idea belongs in this wider NLD language. It treats
computation as controlled symbolic flow, not merely as static symbol
manipulation.

\section{What Is New, and What Is Not}

It is important to be careful.

The following are not new by themselves: binary computation, lookup tables,
optical computing, CCD/CMOS imaging, reservoir computing, photonic
computing, diffractive neural networks, quantum circuit simulation, or parallel
image processing. These existing fields are real and substantial
\cite{Goodman2005,Jaeger2001,Lin2018,Tanaka2019}.

The possible novelty lies in the FSM synthesis. The chapter proposes a
language in which binary computation, quantum computation, and map
computation can be compared as different ways of holding functional
symbolic trajectories.

It also proposes a map-first interpretation of a possible Alphonic engine. The
optics create or activate maps. The detector reads maps. The compression is
held in the stable relation between maps. The computation is the activation of
a preloaded symbolic relation field.

This framing may help avoid two common traps. The first trap is treating
quantum computation as magical parallelism. The second trap is treating
optical computation as if the optical process alone is the whole explanation.
FSM instead asks where the functional symbolic trajectory is held, how it is
preloaded, how it is transformed, how it is measured, and how its uncertainty
is stabilised.

\section{Conclusion: Computation as Controlled Symbolic Unfolding}

The three computational approaches may now be summarised.

Binary computation transforms discrete symbolic states through explicit logic.
Quantum computation preloads a coupled ket trajectory, evolves it under
Hilbert-space rules, and flattens the result through measurement. FSM /
Alphonic map computation preloads relational structure into finite symbolic
maps and activates stable transformations that unfold many symbolic
relations in parallel.

The deeper comparison is not hardware alone. It is symbolic geometry.

Binary computation stores function in instructions, registers, memory, and


logic. Quantum computation stores function in state preparation, ket
representation, coupled evolution, and measurement statistics. FSM map
computation stores function in predefined symbolic maps and the relation
field between them.

The common structure is:
\begin{equation}
\text{preparation}
\rightarrow
\text{transformation}
\rightarrow
\text{measurement}
\rightarrow
\text{symbolic result}.
\end{equation}

The difference lies in where the compression lives. For binary computation,
compression lives in algorithms, instruction sets, memory structures, and logic
circuits. For quantum computation, compression lives in ket representation,
state preparation, unitary transformation, and measurement design. For FSM
map computation, compression lives in stable symbolic maps and their
preloaded relation fields.

This gives the final compressed formulation:
\begin{equation}
\text{Binary computation: compute the relation.}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{Quantum computation: evolve the coupled trajectory.}

Constraint is the local rule structure that keeps the path admissible. This may
be grammar, social agreement, mathematical formalism, experimental
protocol, measurement convention, or the practical rules of a particular
language game.

Local uncertainty is the neighbourhood of possible reconstructions and
continuations surrounding the current symbol. A word always carries nearby
alternatives. It has a region of possible meanings, not a single perfect point.

History is the generative path that brought the symbol into being. Much of
this history is usually compressed or lost once the symbol is instantiated. A
reader sees the word, not the whole path by which the word became useful.

Once symbols are seen in this way, representation and meaning begin to look
less like fixed correspondence and more like a finite geometry of motion. This
leads to the next idea i.e. a new functional symbolic trajectory: the Fractal
Geodesic.

The Fractal Geodesic
When we consider language as a nonlinear dynamical system, a fractal
geodesic is a self-similar bundle of functional symbolic trajectories nested
across multiple scales.

The word fractal is used because the pattern repeats at different scales. A
word has internal history and local uncertainty. A sentence has the same. A
paragraph has the same. A theory has the same. The structure repeats, but
not as a perfect copy. It repeats as a family of related symbolic movements.

The word geodesic is used because a coherent symbolic pathway often
behaves like a path of constrained movement through a space of possibilities.
It is not necessarily the shortest path in a simple geometric sense. It is the
path that holds together under the constraints of the system.

A good sentence is a small geodesic.

A good proof is a formal geodesic.


A good scientific theory is a large geodesic.

A conversation that remains coherent is a coupled geodesic between
speakers.

On Coherence
We can now see that coherence arises when the nested sub-trajectories flow
without destructive conflict. They do not pull the reader into incompatible
basins or introduce hidden assumptions that break the path. Rather, they
remain mutually reinforcing with the larger trajectory they compose. Inversely,
incoherence appears when a sub-trajectory slips its constraint, pulls against
the parent path, or collapses into a nearby attractor that distorts the trajectory
and reduces the functionality. This is why a single word can destabilise a
sentence and why one hidden assumption can weaken a proof. It is also is
why one undefined term can make a theory harder to follow and why an AI
response can begin coherently and then quietly drift into what we call
‘hallucination’.

Historical provenance
The idea presented here did not appear out of nowhere. Rather, it sits inside a
much longer historical trajectory concerning meaning, logic, language,
information, and dynamical systems.

Bertrand Russell, in his work on logical construction, tried to clarify how many
mathematical and conceptual objects could be treated not as directly given
things, but as constructions that allow reliable work to be done. His famous
programme of replacing inferred entities with logical constructions was an
attempt to make the scaffolding more explicit.

In plain terms, Russell was trying to show how some “objects” used in
language and thought may function as useful symbolic constructions rather
than as directly measured things.

This is close to the spirit of the functional symbolic trajectory, although not
quite the same. Russell was still working within the logic and foundations
available to him. He did not have access to the later concepts and vocabulary
of compression, nonlinear embedding, computational language models,
signal reconstruction, or finite measurement geometry. He could see that
some symbolic objects do work, yet could not yet describe them as
trajectories through a dynamical semantic space.

Ludwig Wittgenstein, who studied with Russell, later moved the discussion in
another direction. In his later writings, he described language as a vast
collection of practices, often called language games. Words do not have
meaning only because they point to things. They have meaning because of
how they are used within local forms of life, local rules, and local contexts.

The same word can do different work in different games.

The meaning of a word depends on the path it is travelling.


In the language being developed here, many of Wittgenstein’s observations
can be reinterpreted as observations about functional symbolic trajectories.
He saw that words act at different scales. He saw that meaning depends on
use. He saw that context is not decoration added after the fact. Context is part
of the pathway that makes the word function. This is why a word can be stable
in one setting and unstable in another as the constraint structure has changed
and the trajectory has moved.

Connecting Information to Mathematics
Claude Shannon changed the landscape again in 1948 with his mathematical
theory of communication. Shannon was not trying to solve meaning in the
ordinary human sense as his work was aimed at communication, uncertainty,
coding, transmission, and noise. However, he gave us an engineering
language for thinking about information as something that can be encoded,
compressed, transmitted, corrupted, and recovered. This matters here
because symbolic trajectories are not merely philosophical. They are also
practical as they are compressed, transmitted and reconstructed and they are
also vulnerable to noise and loss.

When a sentence is written, the full living thought that produced it is
compressed into marks. And when a reader reads the sentence, the marks are
expanded again into a reconstructed pathway. The original trajectory is never
perfectly recovered, rather it is measured and measurements have uncertainty:
they are part of an interactive dynamical process of measurement.

The Rise of Nonlinear Dynamical System Theory
Edward Lorenz then showed, in the early 1960s, how deterministic systems
could behave in ways that were highly sensitive to initial conditions. His work
on weather helped establish what later became widely known as chaos theory.
A system could follow rules and still become difficult to predict over time.

This is important for language because language is not just a list of symbols. It
is a sequential system with memory, feedback, instability, and basin changes.
A single phrase can redirect an argument. A small ambiguity can produce a
large interpretive change. A hidden assumption can quietly move the reader
into a different attractor.

Finally, Floris Takens and others developed the modern mathematical toolkit
of phase space reconstruction. Takens’ embedding theorem showed that the
hidden dynamics of a system can, under certain conditions, be reconstructed
from a measured time series. This is one of the key bridges.

Language arrives as a time series. Speech arrives through time.

Text is processed through sequence.

AI models generate tokens sequentially.

Meaning and representation, therefore, can be approached as a reconstructed
dynamical pathway rather than as a static lookup between words and


definitions. From the trajectory being shown here we can see the chain of
provenance of these ideas:

Russell gives part of the logical provenance

Wittgenstein gives part of the use-based provenance.

Shannon gives the communication and compression provenance.

Lorenz gives the nonlinear dynamical provenance.

Takens gives the reconstruction and embedding provenance.

Now in the modern age of AI, large language models now make the whole
issue visible in a new way, because they generate symbolic trajectories in front
of us, one token at a time and this gives us new instruments to measure the
trajectories of language.

The fractal scale in practice
Once we have the unit, the functional symbolic trajectory, we begin to see
fractal geodesics everywhere.

Everyday language
Consider a simple sentence:

“The bank is closed.”

This is one visible trajectory inside the dynamical time series of reading or
listening. Inside it sit smaller sub-trajectories. The word “bank” carries more
than one basin. It may mean a financial institution or it may mean the side of
a river. The word “closed” also constrains the interpretation. The reader’s prior
context adds further constraint. For example, if the previous sentence
mentioned money, accounts, or opening hours, the financial basin becomes
stronger. If the previous sentence mentioned fishing, flooding, or walking
beside a river, the riverside basin becomes stronger.

The sentence feels coherent when the sub-trajectories converge without
conflict and it becomes unstable when they pull in different directions. Now
consider:

“The bank is sky.”

or:

“The bank is driving.”

Here the reader feels friction. The trajectory does not settle easily. The words
can be parsed grammatically, but their functional pathways do not naturally
reinforce one another. The reader searches for a metaphor, a joke, a mistake,
a surreal frame, or some missing context. That search is not incidental; it is the


dynamical reconstruction of meaning in action. We feel logic when the
trajectory holds and confusion when the trajectory loses admissibility.

Mathematics
Importantly, mathematics does not stand outside language. Mathematics is
supported by language, notation, axioms, rule systems, historical agreement,
training, and shared procedures of use. It is a highly constrained language,
but still symbolic and sequential.

A proof can be therefore be treated as an example of a fractal geodesic. Each
definition, lemma, equation, substitution, transformation, and conclusion is a
sub-trajectory operating under strict formal constraint. The proof remains
coherent only while every sub-trajectory stays nested inside the parent rule
structure.

A single illegal move breaks the path.

A hidden assumption changes the basin.

A poorly defined term adds uncertainty.

A symbol introduced without constraint becomes unstable.

In considering proofs as functional symbolic trajectories, this does not weaken
mathematics, rather it helps explain mathematics’ power. Mathematics works
so well because it creates highly disciplined symbolic trajectories. It restricts
the possible moves and compresses prior work into reusable forms. It then
allows readers trained in the same system to reconstruct the pathway with
unusually low uncertainty. However, even in mathematics, the symbols are not
magic. They still require admissible use.

A proof is not merely a collection of marks, it is a path that can be followed
and a functional symbolic trajectory.

Large language models
For those that have followed my work, large language models make
functional symbolic trajectories unusually visible. An AI-generated response
unfolds one token at a time and each token is selected from a local region of
possible continuations. The model does not produce a complete meaning all
at once. Rather, it generates a pathway through a high-dimensional space of
learned relations. From this perspective we can see that each next-token
choice can be seen as a micro-trajectory.

The whole response is coherent when those micro-trajectories remain nested
inside the constraint basin established by the prompt, the prior context, the
training distribution, and the local structure of the response. ‘Hallucination’ is
what it looks like when one or more sub-trajectories drift. For example:

A name may be generated that fits the local linguistic shape but not the
external record.


A citation may sound plausible but fail as a measurement.

An argument may remain fluent while its constraint structure has already
slipped.

The trajectory of language reconstruction continues, but the geodesic has
crossed into a conflicting basin. This is why AI hallucination is perhaps seen
not only as an error of fact but also a trajectory error where the symbolic path
remains locally smooth while losing contact with the intended constraint.

It seems to me this also explains why when interacting with both an AI or a
person the language we use needs great care. Each sentence does not simply
ask for a response, it shapes the basin of possible continuation and constrains
the trajectory before it begins.

Physics and metrology
Continuing the functional symbolic trajectory presented here; physics and
metrology provide another example. The modern definition of the SI second,
from the International System of Units, is a textbook case of a metrological
fractal geodesic.

It begins with a physical process, the caesium-133 hyperfine transition. It then
moves through a counted relation, 9 192 631 770 periods. That relation
becomes a formal symbolic definition. The definition becomes realised in
instruments and the instruments then support clocks. Finally, the clocks
support laboratories, navigation systems, software, engineering, publications,
and downstream physical models.

Each layer is a functional symbolic trajectory operating at a different scale.

The physical transition is one trajectory.

The counting convention is another.

The formal definition is another.

The laboratory realisation is another.

The software implementation is another.

The textbook explanation is another.

The entire construct remains coherent only while the nested trajectories
remain anchored to the originating measurement process and to the agreed
metrological procedure. Importantly, this does not make the SI second unreal.
Quite the opposite as it shows how much work is required to make it stable.
We can see that a unit of measurement is not merely a word. It is a
maintained symbolic trajectory held in place by instruments, institutions,
definitions, histories, and repeated use.

The same applies more broadly in physics. A published result is not just a


number. Rather, it is a pathway from apparatus, calibration, measurement,
modelling, statistical treatment, notation, interpretation, and social
acceptance. When that pathway is clear, the result becomes easier to evaluate
and when parts of the pathway are hidden, compressed, or smuggled in as
assumptions, the functional symbolic trajectory starts to become unstable.

Does any of this matter?
For me, the answer is yes. The tools that make these trajectories visible are
relatively new. Compression algorithms, signal theory, nonlinear embedding,
large language models, precise metrological anchoring, and modern
computation have changed what we can see. Older philosophy often treated
symbols as static pointers, names, propositions, or logical objects. That work
remains important, and started the trajectory presented here, however, we are
no longer limited to that framing and can ask a more practical question. Not
only, “What does this symbol mean?” but also: “How is the symbol situated on
a finite symbolic pathway?”

Within the context of both science and AI this shift matters as it may deliver
diagnostic tools. For example, when reading a scientific paper, we can ask
where the main functional symbolic trajectories are and which definitions are
doing the heavy work. We can ask whether the mathematical pathway stays
inside its stated constraints and where an interpretation is added. We can ask
whether a term is stable or whether it carries unresolved local uncertainty.

Then when debugging an AI output, we can ask where the response first
drifted and which sub-trajectory may have crossed into a neighbouring basin.
Finally, we can ask whether the output remained fluent after it had already
lost constraint.

It also seems to me that it then follows, that when developing a new
theoretical term, we can ask whether the term has enough history, constraint,
and use to hold meaning. This may help to question whether it is a premature
noun, a slow noun, or a stabilising pathway that may become useful through
repeated measurement and refinement.

Importantly, the term “functional symbolic trajectory” is itself a slow noun. My
hope is that its meaning will sharpen through use. It is offered for others to
test and stabilise, and as a working expression for something that appears
across language, mathematics, AI, metrology, and scientific modelling. It
names the finite symbolic pathway that allows meaning to be constructed and
for me helps explain why some arguments hold together and others drift. It
also may help explain why words can be powerful, unstable, compressed,
inherited, and dangerous. It also helps explain why a symbol is not merely a
mark, but needs a path.

What are the visible functional symbolic trajectories here?

of the concept, it gives us a way to see the motion - that is to say the
dynamics of a living language that can never be trapped in stone.
