1. The Distinction That Was Not Deep Enough
In the earlier essay, we described the comfort of nouns. We name a river, and
by naming it we stabilise something that is never still. The water changes. The
banks erode. The channel shifts. The ecological, geological, and human
histories of the river continue to unfold. Yet the noun “river” works. It is not a
mistake. It is an astonishing compression.

At first, this seems to suggest a simple opposition: nouns freeze; verbs flow.
But this opposition is not deep enough. A river is not a noun pretending to
hide a verb. A river is a verb whose dynamics are slow enough, stable enough,
and recurrent enough to permit naming. The noun does not stand outside the
process. It is one way the process appears when observed through a finite
window.

Language is forever changing adapting even nouns. The river flows quickly as
water, slowly as channel, more slowly as valley, and more slowly still as a
feature in cultural memory. The same “thing” is not one thing at all. It is a
nested set of rates. So the distinction between noun and verb begins to
dissolve.

A verb is a visible motion. A noun is a motion whose change has fallen below
the immediate threshold of observation.

The noun is the slow verb.

2. Rate, Not Category
The classical grammatical distinction between noun and verb is useful. It
allows sentences to be formed, taught, parsed, and translated. It is one of the
great instruments of human symbolic life. But like all instruments, it also
imposes a resolution.

Consider a child learns that “stone” is a noun and “falling” is a verb. This is
helpful. Yet the stone is not static. It is weathering, vibrating, exchanging heat,
being chemically altered, existing within gravitational and electromagnetic
interactions. At a longer time scale, the stone becomes dust. At a still longer
time scale, it participates in geological flow.

The falling is merely the stone’s motion seen within a human-sized interval.
The stone is motion seen at another rate. What we call an object is often a


process whose rate of change is slow relative to the organism naming it. What
we call an action is often a process whose rate of change is fast enough to
enter awareness as motion. This suggests a more general rule:

The apparent category of a word depends on the measurement window
through which its referent is sampled.

A mountain is a noun to a human life yet it is a verb to geology. A species is a
noun to a textbook yet it is a verb to evolution.

A self is a noun in ordinary speech.
It is a verb in memory, metabolism, habit, language, and relation.

A philosophy is a noun in a catalogue and a verb in the life of thought. This is
part of the reason often callGeofinitism a living philosophy. That phrase now
seems more exact than first realised. Geofinitism is not only a philosophy
about finite measurement. It is itself a continuing act of measurement,
correction, perturbation, and re-stabilisation. It does not merely describe
dynamics, it proceeds dynamically. Perhaps, then, Geofinitism is not best
understood as a noun. Perhaps Geofinitism is a itself a verb.

3. The Slow Verb and the Measurement Window
To say that nouns are slow verbs is not merely a poetic inversion. It is a
statement about measurement. Every act of understanding occurs within a
finite window. We do not perceive all scales at once; we observe locally,
bodily, historically, culturally, and instrumentally. A word becomes stable
when the changes it conceals are smaller than the resolution of the system
using it. And from this perspective, naming is a sampling operation.

The word “tree” gathers root exchange, photosynthesis, growth, decay, fungal
relation, seasonal variation, cellular repair, and ecological interaction into a
single stable symbol. It does not eliminate those processes, it hides them
beneath a low-rate surface. The noun is therefore not false. It is finite and a
compression shaped by the limits of the observer.

From this perspective this explains why nouns are so powerful as they reduce
the cognitive burden of the world. Without them, experience would remain
too fluid to manage. To live, we must stabilise and in speaking, we must
compress. To reason, we must hold something still long enough to compare it
with something else. However, the concern comes when we forget that the
stillness was introduced by the instrument. The noun did not reveal the final
nature of the world, it does however give the mind a handle.


The Drift of Meaning Across Time
If nouns are slow verbs, then their motion is not always visible within a single
lifetime. Yet over longer intervals, the movement becomes unmistakable.
Words that appear stable within one generation can shift, stretch, fracture, or
invert across decades and centuries. What appears fixed is often only locally
stable.

Consider the word “awful.” In earlier English, it meant “full of awe,” carrying a
sense closer to reverence or wonder. Over time, its trajectory shifted toward
the negative, until in contemporary usage it signifies something unpleasant or
bad. The word did not break; it drifted. The apparent object remained, but its
direction of meaning rotated.

A similar transformation can be seen in “silly.” Once meaning “blessed” or
“innocent,” it moved through stages of “naive” and “harmless” before settling
into its current sense of foolishness. At no point was there a sharp boundary
at which the word ceased to be itself. Instead, its meaning flowed through
intermediate states, each locally intelligible, yet collectively forming a
trajectory that would be difficult to reconstruct without historical awareness.

Other words shift less in tone and more in conceptual structure. The word
“science” in the 17th century referred broadly to organised knowledge. What
we now call “science” would then have been described more precisely as
natural philosophy. Over time, the term narrowed and stabilised around
experimental method, measurement, and institutional practice. A reader
approaching a 17th-century text with the modern sense of “science” risks
importing assumptions that were not present in the original context.

The word “extent,” used in early modern philosophy, offers a more subtle
example. It did not merely refer to size or range in the modern sense, but
carried connotations of spatial being—what it is for something to occupy
space. In texts influenced by thinkers such as René Descartes, “extension” was
not a casual descriptor but a foundational term in the attempt to define
matter itself. To read such passages with a contemporary, casual sense of
“extent” is to flatten a concept that was once structurally central.

Some words shift rapidly under cultural pressure. The word “gay,” within the
span of a few decades in the 20th century, moved from “carefree” or “light-
hearted” to its now primary association with sexual identity. The earlier
meaning has not entirely vanished, but it has receded to the point where its
use may cause confusion or require explanation.

More recent still is the word “cloud.” Once referring almost exclusively to a


meteorological formation, it has, within a few decades, come to signify
distributed computational infrastructure. The older meaning remains, but the
newer one has become dominant in technical and professional contexts. The
same symbol now operates across distinct domains, with interpretation
determined by context.

Even the word “computer” has undergone a reversal. Originally referring to a
person who performs calculations, it now refers almost exclusively to a
machine. The noun has not simply shifted; its referent has been reassigned
entirely.

These examples are not anomalies. They are expressions of a general
condition: words move. Their trajectories are shaped by use, context, culture,
technology, and institutional reinforcement. A word may appear stable within
a given measurement window, yet across a longer interval, its motion
becomes visible. The consequence for reading is significant. When
approaching a historical text, the reader often assumes that the words carry
their current meanings. This assumption is rarely examined. Yet it introduces a
systematic distortion. The reader overlays a contemporary trajectory onto an
earlier one, producing a hybrid meaning that belongs fully to neither.

In this sense, interpretation is itself a measurement problem. The question is
not only what does the word mean, but what did the word do at the time of
its use. What trajectory did it participate in? What alternatives surrounded it?
What distinctions did it carry that may now have dissolved?

To read carefully, then, is not merely to parse sentences, but to attend to the
temporal behaviour of words. It is to recognise that the noun on the page is
not a fixed object transported intact through history. It is a slowed process,
observed at a different point along its path. The word you encounter is not
the word that was written. It is the current position of a trajectory that began
elsewhere and will continue beyond the present.

6. Semantic Uncertainty
In geofinitism semantic uncertainty is the uncertainty associated with the
meaning of a word, phrase, or symbolic structure under finite interpretation,
and ‘endogenous measurment.

It is not vagueness or ambiguity. It is the measurable instability of meaning as
a word travels through different readers, contexts, traditions, and histories.

A word with low semantic uncertainty remains relatively stable under
reasonable perturbation.

A word with high semantic uncertainty shifts strongly depending on who uses
it, where it appears, and what basin of thought the reader already inhabits.

In this reading, semantic uncertainty is not a defect in language. It is part of
language’s dynamical life. Poetry often uses high semantic uncertainty
deliberately and myth often depends on it. Religious language often lives
within it and it seems to me philosophy has often cultivated it. Even science
uses high-uncertainty words during periods of discovery, before measurement
stabilises the domain.


But if the aim is careful inquiry, then high semantic uncertainty must be
acknowledged, constrained, or reduced. A Geofinitist soft rule may therefore
be proposed:

Addendum: Language Under Compression
LLMs and the Dynamics of Meaning
There is a further development that must now be considered. It arises not

instruments are Large Language Models.

are verb-like systems. Yet there is a second effect, less immediately visible.

The training of such systems is an act of compression. Vast corpora of human
language, themselves the accumulated trajectories of culture, are reduced into
a finite set of parameters. This compression is not neutral. It preserves some
structures more strongly than others. Frequent patterns are reinforced. Rare
trajectories are attenuated. Ambiguity is smoothed. Instability is regularised.

What is learned is not the full dynamical richness of language, but a weighted
reconstruction of it. This introduces a new pressure on the evolution of
language itself. When such systems are used to generate text, assist writing,
or mediate communication, they begin to feed back into the very corpus from
which they were trained. Human language, once an open and uneven field of
perturbation, increasingly encounters its own compressed reflection. A loop is
formed:

Language produces models.
Models produce language.
Language absorbs the model’s output.
The cycle continues.

Within this loop, the attractor landscape of language may begin to shift.
Stable patterns deepen. Common forms become more common. Certain
phrasings gain authority through repetition. Unusual trajectories may struggle
to persist unless actively maintained. In the terms developed in this essay, we
may say:


Systems built upon the dynamics of language may, in their outputs, favour the
slow verb.

That is, they may preferentially generate trajectories that are already stable,
already well-formed, already close to existing attractors. The result is language
that is coherent, fluent, and often useful, but also subtly constrained. This
does not imply a loss of intelligence or capability. It implies a redistribution of
dynamical emphasis.

Language may become, on average, more regular. More predictable. More
aligned with established basins. The faster, less stable trajectories—the
experimental, the idiosyncratic, the not-yet-formed—may require greater
effort to sustain. They may not disappear, but they may become less likely to
arise spontaneously.

This presents both a risk and an opportunity.

The risk is a gradual slowing of linguistic evolution.

Not a cessation, but a drift toward deeper, more stable basins at the expense
of exploratory movement. A culture that increasingly speaks through its own
compressed representations may begin to mistake coherence for
completeness.

The opportunity lies in awareness.

If language is indeed a dynamical system, and if these new instruments
participate in its evolution, then it becomes possible to study, measure, and
guide this process. We may begin to ask:

What trajectories are being reinforced?

Which are being lost?

How does semantic uncertainty change under model mediation?

Does the use of such systems reduce or increase the diversity of expression?

Do they deepen existing basins or help reveal new ones?
